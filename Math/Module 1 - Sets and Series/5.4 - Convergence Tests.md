# 5.4 - Convergence Tests

It's not always practical to find an expression for $S_k$ for an infinite series and take it's limit. Let's explore some easier ways to test if a series converges or diverges.

## Divergence Test

> [!theorem] The Divergence Test
> $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}$ is a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^divergent-series]] if the [[3.4 - Limits#^limit]] $\lim_{n\to\infty}{a_{n}}$ is nonzero, or does not exist.
 $$\lim_{n \to \infty}a_{n}\neq0\;\;\text{or}\,\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}\text{ DNE}$$

^e820ea

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^e820ea]]
The divergence test can only test for divergence. Realize that an infinite series can only converge if each progressive term gets smaller. If each term gets bigger approaching infinity, then the infinite sum must go to infinity as well. Otherwise, you're adding an infinite number of finitely large things!
`\end{proof}`

## Integral Test

> [!theorem] The Integral Test
> Let $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}$ where $a_{n}>0$. Let there be a function $f$ and $N\in \mathbb{Z}^{+}$ such that:
> 1. $f$ is continuous,
> 2. $f$ is decreasing, and
> 3. $f(n) = a_n$ for all integers $n\geq N$
> 
> Then, $$\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}\text{ and }\int_{N}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx $$
> are both [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^convergent-series]] or both [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^divergent-series]].
>

^the-integral-test

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^the-integral-test]]
Recall that an indefinite integral can be thought of as the **Reimann sum**, or the sum of infinitely many rectangles under a curve. This is just another way of rephrasing an infinite series (a sum of infinitely many terms). This sum doesn't have to start from $1$ -- it could start from any positive integer $N$.

So for an infinite series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}$, consider the curve of $f(x)=a_{n}$. We can see that the integral $\int_{1}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx$ is an **upper bound** for the infinite series.

![[Pasted image 20250506210636.png]]

So if the integral converges/diverges, then the series must converge/diverge respectively. Notice that by placing bounds on the series, we also place bounds on it's **sequence of partial sums**. We can use this to prove it's convergence/divergence with the [[5.1 - Infinite Sequences#^monotone-convergence-theorem]]
`\end{proof}`

### Remainder Estimate

> [!def] Remainder of an Infinite Series
> For $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}=S$ and a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^partial-sum]] $S_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^Na_{n}$, the **remainder** $R_N$ is the difference between the value of an infinite series and the value of a partial sum.
> $$
> R_{N}=S-S_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}-\sum_{n=1}^Na_{n}=a_{N+1}+a_{N+2}+\cdots
> $$

^remainder-of-an-infinite-series

> [!thm] The Remainder Estimate
> Let $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}$ where $a_{n}>0$. Let there be a function $f$ and a positive integer $N$ such that:
> 1. $f$ is continuous,
> 2. $f$ is decreasing, and
> 3. $f(n) = a_n$ for all integers $n\geq N$
> 
> Then, the [[#^remainder-of-an-infinite-series]] satisfies the following estimate:
> $$\int_{N+1}^{\infty}f(x)\, dx<R_{N}<\int_{N}^{\infty}f(x)\, dx$$
>
>
>

^the-remainder-estimate

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^the-remainder-estimate]]
To estimate the remainder, let's look at the improper integral of $a_n$ again. Assuming all the requirements for the integral test, let $f(n)=a_{n}$. The integral $\int_{N}^{\infty}f(x)  \, dx$ places an upper bound on $a_{N}+a_{N+1}+a_{N+2}+\dots$ and the integral $\int_{N+1}^{\infty}f(x)  \, dx$ places a lower bound on $a_{N}+a_{N+1}+a_{N+2}+\dots$

![[Pasted image 20250507182229.png]]

*On the right, $\int_{N}^{\infty}f(x)  \, dx$, and on the left, $\int_{N+1}^{\infty}f(x)  \, dx$. They place a lower and upper bound respectively.*

So we can estimate $R_n$ to be between these two.
`\end{proof}`

## Comparison Tests

We can often find if an infinite series converges by comparing it to another infinite series.

### Direct Comparison Test

> [!thm] Direct Comparison Test
> Consider every term of $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}$ and $\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n$ after some index $N\in \mathbb{Z}^+$. If the bigger series **converges**, so will the smaller series. If the smaller series **diverges**, so must the bigger series.
>
> - If $\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n$ is a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^convergent-series]] and $0\leq a_{n}\leq b_{n},\ \forall n\geq N$then $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{n}$ also **converges**.
> - Otherwise, if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_{n}$ is a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^divergent-series]] and $a_{n}\geq b_{n}\geq0,\ \forall n\geq N$ then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_{n}$ also **diverges**.

### Limit Comparison Test

> [!thm] Limit Comparison Test
> Consider the limit of the ratios of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}$ where $\forall n\geq 1,\; a_{n}, \,b_{n}\geq 0$.
> $$\lim_{ n \to \infty } \frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}=L$$
> - If $L\neq0$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_{n}$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_{n}$ are **both [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^convergent-series]]** or **both [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^divergent-series]].**
> - If $L=0$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_{n}$ converges if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_{n}$ converges.
> - If $L=\infty$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_{n}$ diverges if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_{n}$ diverges.

^limit-comparison-test

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^limit-comparison-test]]
Consider, for example, the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^{n}-1}$. As $n$ approaches infinity, the minus one in the denominator shouldn't matter much at all, because $2^n$ will be huge. So it's reasonable to think that if the simpler series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}$ converges, than $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n-1}$ should also converge.

To generalize this further, for two series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_{n}$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_{n}$, consider the **ratio** of each term $\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}$. For the example above, that's $\frac{2^n}{2^n -1}$. Now consider what happens to the **limit** of that ratio as $n$ approaches infinity.

If the ratio goes to some **finite constant $C$**, it means that that they **remain proportional** to each other. So they must **converge/diverge together**. If the ratio goes to **zero**, then the two series **basically become equal** as $n$ approaches infinity. So if one converges, so must the other. If the ratio goes to **infinity**, then if one diverges, so must the other, since one is basically infinitely bigger than the other for a large enough $n$. For the above example,

$$
\lim_{ n \to \infty } \frac{2^n}{2^n-1}=1
$$and we know $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}$ converges, so $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n-1}$ must also converge.
`\end{proof}`

## Alternating Series Test

This test is useful for finding out of an alternating series converges. To apply the test, consider what happens to the size of each consecutive term as $n$ approaches infinity. If the terms get smaller and smaller, approaching $0$, then the series should converge. Since the magnitude of each term is determined by $b_{n}$, we just need to consider $\lim_{ n \to \infty }b_{n}$.

> [!theorem] Alternating Series Test
> An [[5.2 - Types of Series#^alternating-series]] is a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^convergent-series]] if
> - $b_{n}$ is decreasing $\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}^+$
> - $\lim_{n\to\infty}{b_{n}}=0$.
> 
>If the limit is not zero, the test **fails**. We can then use the [[5.4 - Convergence Tests#^e820ea]] to show that $\lim_{n\to\infty}{(-1)^{n+1}b_{n}}\;\text{DNE}$, and so the series diverges.

## Ratio Test

Consider the limit of the ratio of consecutive terms $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}$. If that limit is **less than 1**, it means that the terms are getting progressively **smaller** and the series will **absolutely converge**. If that limit is **infinity**, it means the terms are getting progressively **bigger** and the series will **diverge**.

> [!thm] Ratio Test
> For an [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^infinite-series]] with nonzero terms, let $\rho$ be the ratio of consecutive terms.
> $$\rho=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\left\lvert {\frac{a_{n+1}}{a^n}} \right\rvert }$$
> - If $0\leq \rho\leq{1}$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}$ is an [[5.2 - Types of Series#^absolutely-convergent-series|Absolutely Convergent Series]].
> - If $\rho>1$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}$ is a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^divergent-series]].
> - If $\rho=1$, then the test does not provide any information.

^ratio-test

The ratio test is always **inconclusive** for a **$p$-series**. because the limit evaluates to $1$. It's good for series with **factorials** or **exponentials** because the limit is often easy to evaluate.
