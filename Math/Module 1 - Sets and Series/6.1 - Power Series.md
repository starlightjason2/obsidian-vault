# 6.1 - Power Series

A power series is a type of series with terms involving a variable, kind of like an infinite polynomial function. Power series are used to represent common functions and also to define new functions.

> [!def] Power Series
> A power series centered at $a$ is an [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^07b41c]] polynomial of the form
> $$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(x-a)^n=c_{0}+c_{1}(x-a)+c_{2}(x-a)^2+\dots$$
> where $c_{n}$ are constants and $x$ is a variable.

^ac9505

So, for example,
$$f(x)=1+x+x^2+\dots$$
is a power series where $c_{n}=1$ and $a=0$. A more complicated example is

$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\left(\frac{n^5}{n!}\right)(x-3)^n}=0+\frac{1^5}{1!}(x-3)+\frac{2^5}{2!}(x-3)^2+\dots 
$$
^9f368b

where $c_{n}=\frac{n^5}{n!}$ and $a=3$.

## Convergence

Notice when $x=a$ the series evaluates to $c_{0}$ because every term evaluates to $0$, meaning a power series always converges at $x=a$.

We can also see that if $c_{n}$ shrinks much faster than $(x-a)^n$ then a power series will also converge, like in example [[#^9f368b]] 

So we have at least two scenarios - either a series converges only at $x=a$ or for all $x\in \mathbb{R}$.

Let's assume $c_{n}=1$ for simplicity. Notice that now the power series has a form similar to a [[5.2 - Types of Series#^50c439]] where $r=x-a$. Per the rules for [[5.2 - Types of Series#^524c80]], the power series will converge when $\lvert{x-a}\rvert<1 \implies -1<x-a<1$ or

$$
a-1<x<a+1
$$

^8e5157

### Testing Convergence with the Ratio Test

For a more thorough analysis, we can apply the [[Examples#^2dc2d0]]. Let's evaluate the limit of the ratio of consecutive terms for $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}(x-a)^n$.
$$
\begin{align}
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\left\lvert {\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}} \right\rvert }&=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\left\lvert {\frac{c_{n+1}(x-a)^{n+1}}{c_{n}(x-a)^n}} \right\rvert } \\ \\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\left\lvert {\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_{n}}}(x-a) \right\rvert } \\ \\
&=\lvert{x-a}\rvert \lim_{n\to\infty}{\left\lvert {\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_{n}}} \right\rvert }
\end{align}
$$
If $x=a$ then this evaluates to $0$ and the series is **absolutely convergent** by the ratio test, confirming what we produced earlier. Let's continue by assuming this limit converges, because otherwise the power series is **divergent** for all $x\neq a$ and we're done. Let's say that
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\left\lvert {\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_{n}}} \right\rvert }=L
$$
So by the ratio test, the series is convergent if
$$
\begin{align}
L\lvert{x-a}\rvert &<1 \\ \\
\lvert{x-a}\rvert &<\frac{1}{L} \\ \\
-\frac{1}{L}<x-a&<\frac{1}{L} \\ \\
\end{align}
$$
Which finally leaves us with a statement for the **interval of $x$** for which $f(x)$ is **convergent**.

$$
a-\frac{1}{L}<x<a+\frac{1}{L}
$$

This is a more general form of [[#^8e5157]], and it tells us that the power series converges within some **radius of convergence** $R=\frac{1}{L}$ of the **center** $x=a$.

> [!def] Radius of Convergence
> If a [[#^ac9505]] falls into Case #3 of [[#^c09947]] then we call $R$ the radius of convergence, where $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(x-a)^n$ **converges** for $a-R<x<a+R$.

^e0f9cf

The **endpoints** $x=a\pm R$ are **may not be included** in this interval, so they must be **tested separately**.

Our final three scenarios look like this, and can be combined into a single theorem.

![[Pasted image 20250519193335.png]]

> [!thm] Convergence of Power Series
> Use the following rules to determine whether a [[#^ac9505]] $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}(x-a)^n$ is a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^9287fb]] or a [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^30d997]] . The series is either
> 1. **Convergent** for all $x\in \mathbb{R}$,
> 2. **Convergent** when $x=a$, **divergent** when $x\neq a$,
> 3. **Convergent** when $x \in (a-R,\ a+R)$, **divergent** when $x \notin (a-R,\ a+R)$, and **unknown** when $x \in \{a-R,\ a+R\}$.

^c09947

## Functions Represented by Power Series

Power series are very useful for representing all sorts of complicated functions. Because of their polynomial nature, they're easy to differentiate, integrate, and manipulate in other ways.

To see this intuitively, let's explore what a simple power series looks like.
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$$
This is also a geometric series with $a=1,\ r=x$. So over the interval of convergence, when $|x|<1,\ -1<x<1$, the series will converge to
$$p(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}$$
per the [[5.2 - Types of Series#^524c80]].

Let's compare this to the [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^e2db1a]] $p_{n}$ of the power series,
$$
\begin{align}
p_{n}(x)&=\sum_{i=0}^nx^i \implies\\ \\ 
p_{0}(x)&=1 \\
p_{1}(x)&=1+x  \\
p_{2}(x)&=1+x+x^2 \\
p_{3}(x)&=1+x+x^2+x^3 \\
&\dots
\end{align}
$$
The partial sums are slowly approaching the value of the infinite series, something we can see graphically if we plot partial sums for increasing values of $n$ (blue) against the infinite series (red).

```desmos-graph
left=-1.5; right=1.5;
top=3; bottom=-0.5;
---
y=1/(1-x)  | red
y=1 | blue | dashed
y=1+x | blue | dashed
y=1+x+x^2 | blue | dashed
y=1+x+x^2+x^3 | blue | dashed
-1<x<1
```

The partial sums become a better fit for the infinite series as $n$ increases, but they only seem to fit within the highlighted interval of convergence. That makes sense, since the power series diverge outside of it. This means that within that interval, we can represent the function $f(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}$ as the infinite power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$.

$$
f(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n,\ -1<x<1
$$
^afec8e

## Combining Power Series

We can add or subtract power series with the **same interval of convergence**, just like any other convergent series. We can also multiply power series by a factor, which simply scales their convergence function by that factor. Finally, we can compose power series with another function, and simply bass the composition function into the original output function.

> [!thm] Combining Power Series
> Let $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^n$ and $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}x^n$ be two [[6.1 - Power Series#^ac9505]] that converge to functions $f$ and $g$ over a **common interval** $I$. For any $m\in \mathbb{Z}^+, \ b\in \mathbb{R}$,
> 1. $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(c_{n}x^n\pm d_{n}x^n)$ converges to $f\pm g$ over $I$.
> 2. $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}bx^mc_{n}x^n$ converges to $bx^mf(x)$ over $I$,
> 3. $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(bx^m)^n$ converges to $f(bx^m)$ for all $bx^m\in I$

### Multiplying Power Series

Since a power series is just an infinite polynomial, we can multiply two power series the same way we multiply two polynomials.

$$
\begin{align}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^n&=c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^2+\dots \\
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}x^n&=d_{0}+d_{1}x+d_{2}x^2+\dots \\
\left( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^n \right)\left( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}x^n \right)&=(c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^2+\dots )(d_{0}+d_{1}x+d_{2}x^2+\dots ) \\
&=c_{0}d_{0}+(c_{1}d_{0}+c_{0}d_{1})\,x+(c_{2}d_{0}+c_{1}d_{1}+c_{0}d_{2})\,x^2+\dots
\end{align}
$$
This result is called the Cauchy product, and it creates a third infinite series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^nx^n$. Notice that the coefficients of the product follow a pattern -- Each coefficient $e_{n}$ is a miniature series in-and-of-itself!
$$
\begin{align}
e_{n}&=c_{0}d_{n}+c_{1}d_{n-1}+c_{2}d_{n-2}+\dots+c_{n-1}d_{1}+c_{n}d_{0} \\
e_{n}&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}d_{n-k}
\end{align}
$$
The Cauchy product is a sort of nested series where each term contains another (finite) series. So the first coefficient has one term, the second coefficient has two terms, and the $n$th coefficient has $n$ terms.

> [!def] Cauchy Product
> Let $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^n$ and $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}x^n$ be two [[6.1 - Power Series#^ac9505]] that converge to functions $f$ and $g$ over a **common interval** $I$.
>
> Let $e_{n}$ be the [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^e2db1a]]
> $$\begin{align}
e_{n}&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}d_{n-k} \\
&=c_{0}d_{n}+c_{1}d_{n-1}+c_{2}d_{n-2}+\dots+c_{n-1}d_{1}+c_{n}d_{0}
\end{align}$$
> The **Cauchy Product** of the two power series is another power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e_{n}$ converging to $f(x)g(x)$ over $I$.
>$$
>\left( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^n \right)\left( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}x^n \right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e_{n}x^n
>$$
>

## Calculus of Power Series

Differentiating and integrating power series is very easy (thankfully!). We do it just like any other polynomial, following the sum rules and power rules for differentiation and integration respectively. For a power series
$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^n=c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^2+\dots$$
the coefficients $c_{n}$ are constants, and can be taken out of the derivative/integral. When differentiating, the constant $c_{0}$ will disappear, and the resulting series will start from $n=1$. The application of the power rule is straightforward.

> [!thm] Differentiation and Integration of Power Series
> Let $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(a-x)^n$ be a [[6.1 - Power Series#^ac9505]] with [[6.1 - Power Series#^e0f9cf]] $R$. Let $f(x)$ be the function the series equals over it's interval of convergence. Then, for $x \in(a-R,a+R)$, $f$ is differentiable and
>$$\begin{align}
f'(x)&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}n(x-a)^{n-1} \\
> \int f(x)\,dx&=C+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{n+1}(x-a)^{n+1}
\end{align}$$

### Finding Power Series with Differentials and Integrals

A powerful use case for this is finding power series for non-obvious functions by reducing them to some form of $f(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}$. Let's go through some examples of how to do this.

> [!example] Finding a Power Series through Differentiating
> **Find the power series for**
> $$f(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x)^2}$$
>
>
> Notice that $\frac{d}{dx}\left[  \frac{1}{1-x} \right]=\frac{1}{(1-x)^2}$. Therefore the derivative of the power series $\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$ should equal $f(x)$.
>$$
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{1-x}&=\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n &&=1+x+x^2+x^3+\dots\\
\frac{d}{dx} \left[ \frac{1}{1-x} \right]&=\frac{d}{dx} \left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n \right]&&=\frac{d}{dx} [1+x+x^2+x^3]\\
\frac{1}{(1-x)^2}&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nx^{n-1}&&=1+2x+3x^2+\dots \\
\end{align}
>$$
>Nothing we did will change the interval of convergence, but we still need to test the endpoints individually. 
>$$\begin{align}
x&=1&\to&&&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n(1)^{n-1} \\
> x&=-1&\to&&&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n(-1)^{n-1}
\end{align}$$
>
Both diverge by the [[5.4 - Convergence Tests#^e820ea]].
>
**The final power series with the interval of convergence is $$
\begin{align}
f(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x)^2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nx^{n-1},\; x\in(-1,1)
\end{align}
$$**
