# 6.2 - Taylor Series

The Taylor Series lets us to find a power series for nearly any function using it's derivatives. This is reminiscent of extrapolating a function using it's tangent line at a value, except now we're also adding the second, third, etc. derivatives.

> [!def] Taylor Series
> The **Taylor series** for a [[3.4 - Limits#^continuous-function]] $f(x)$ is the [[6.1 - Power Series#^power-series]] satisfying [[#^taylors-theorem]]:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f(x) &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n \\
> &=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}+\dotsb
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The Taylor Series at $x=0$ is the **Maclaurin Series**.
>

^taylor-series

> [!thm] Taylor's Theorem
> Any [[3.4 - Limits#^continuous-function]] $f(x)$ that has a [[3.5 - Derivatives#^derivative]] of all orders at $x = a$. The **Taylor series** for $f$ is the [[6.1 - Power Series#^power-series]],
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f(x) &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n \\
> &=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}+\dotsb
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
>

^taylors-theorem

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^taylors-theorem]]
Suppose that $f(x)$ can be expressed as a power series. Then the $n$th derivative of each is also equal.

$$
\begin{align}
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(x-a)^n&\implies \frac{d^{n}f}{dx^{n}}=\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\left[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(x-a)^n \right] 
\end{align}
$$

Evaluated at $x=a$, we see that $c_{0}(0)^{0}+c_{1}(0)^{1}+c_{2}(0)^{2}+\dots=c_{0}$, and so on for each derivative.

$$
\begin{align}
f(a)&=c_{0} \\
f'(a)&=c_{1} (1)\\
f''(a)&=c_{2}(2\cdot 1) \\
f'''(a)&=c_{3}(3\cdot 2\cdot 1) \\
&\dots 
\end{align}
$$

So the $n$th derivative at $x=a$ should be $f^{(n)}(a)=c_{n}\cdot n!$, from which we can solve for $c_{n}$:

$$
c_{n}=\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}
$$
`\end{proof}`

## Approximating Functions

> [!def] Taylor Polynomial
> The $n$th [[5.2 - Infinite Series#^partial-sum]] of the [[#^taylor-series]] of $f$ at $a$ is the $n$th **Taylor polynomial**. The $n$th Taylor polynomial at $x=0$ is the $n$th **Maclaurin polynomial** of $f$.
>$$\begin{align}
p_{n}(x)&=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\frac{f^{(i)}(a)}{i!}(x-a)^{i}} \\
&=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}+\dots+\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^{n}
\end{align}$$

^652e90

We can use Taylor and Maclaurin series to approximate polynomials by taking a partial sum of a Taylor or Maclaurin series. We call that function a Taylor or Maclaurin polynomial.

### Finding Remainders of Taylor Polynomials

> [!theorem] Taylor's Theorem with Remainder
> Let $f$ be a function with $n+1$ derivatives on $x\in I$. Let $p_{n}$ be the $n$th degree [[#^652e90]] of $f$ at $x=a\in I$. Then the $n$th remainder is
> $$R_{n}(x)=f(x)-p_{n}(x)$$
> For $\lvert{x-a}\rvert\leq d$, if the $n$th derivative has an upper bound $\lvert{f^{(n+1)}}\rvert\leq M$ , then the $n$th remainder has an upper bound
> $$\lvert{R_{n}(x)}\rvert \leq M\frac{\lvert{x-a}\rvert^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$$

^taylors-theorem-with-remainder

We can use Taylor's Theorem with Remainder to find the accuracy of a particular $n$th Taylor Polynomial. We simply need to consider the absolute maximum of the $(n+1)$th derivative.

So for a polynomial with $n=2$ terms, we'd consider the bounds of the third derivative $f'''(x)$. We call that absolute maximum $M$. From there, we would substitute $n=2$ and $n+1=3$.

As $x$ moves further from the center (as $\lvert{x-a}\rvert$ increases), we know that the Taylor polynomial will become more inaccurate. So as $\lvert{x-a}\rvert$ increases, $R_{n}$ also increases. Furthermore, as we take higher degree polynomials ($n$ increases), the error should decrease. So as $n$ increases, $R_{n}$ decreases. This is why we see $(n+1)!$ in the denominator and $\lvert{x-a}\rvert$ in the numerator.

## Common Series

> [!def] Binomial Series
> For any $r\in \mathbb{R}$, The [[6.2 - Taylor Series#^taylor-series]] for $f(x)=(1+x)^{r}$ is the **Binomial Series**.
>$$\begin{align}
(1+x)^{r}&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\binom{r}{n}x^{n}} \\
&=1+rx+\frac{(r)(r-1)}{2!}x^{2}+\dots+\frac{r(r-1)\dots(r-n+1)}{n!}x^{n}
\end{align}$$

^0bbd72

> [!exm] Taylor Series for $\frac{1}{1-x}$
> $$\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{x^{n}},\;x\in(-1,1)$$

> [!exm] Taylor Series for $\ln(1+x)$
> $$\ln(1+x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n+1}x^{n+1}}=\frac{x}{1}-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{x^{3}}{3}-\dots,\;x\in(-1,1]$$

> [!exm] Taylor Series for $\arctan$
> $$\arctan(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2n+1}x^{2n+1}},\;x\in[-1,1]$$

> [!exm] Taylor Series for $e^{x}$
> $$e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{x^{n}}{n!}}=1+\frac{x}{1!}+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+\dots,\;x\in[-\infty,\infty]$$

^ef8304

> [!exm] Taylor Series for $\cos$
> $$\cos(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n}}=1-\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+\frac{x^{4}}{4!}-\dots,\;x\in[-\infty,\infty]$$

^taylor-series-for-cos

> [!exm] Taylor Series for $\sin$
> $$\sin(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)!}x^{2n+1}}=x-\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+\frac{x^{5}}{5!},\;x\in[-\infty,\infty]$$

^taylor-series-for-sin

From these, we can find the power series of composite functions by simply replacing $x$ with the function. We can find the power series for a function multiplied by another function if we can convert one of the factors into a power series and distribute the other factor into the summand.

## Solving Differential Equations with Power Series

Taylor Series are a power tool for solving differential equations. Consider the simple differential equation

$$
y'=y
$$

^ec9933

Let's represent both sides as Maclaurin Series.
$$
\begin{align}
y&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{c_{n}x^{n}} \\
y'&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{c_{n}\cdot nx^{n-1}}
\end{align}
$$
We can now plug these two into [[#^ec9933]].

$$
\begin{align}
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{c_{n}\cdot nx^{n-1}}&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{c_{n}x^{n}} \\ \\
c_{1}+(2c_{2})x+(3c_{3})x^{2}+(4c_{4})x^{3}+\dots&=c_{0}+(c_{1})x+(c_{2})x^{2}+(c_{3})x^{3}+(c_{4})x^{4}
\end{align}
$$
Notice that both series are of the same form but with different coefficients for each power of $x$. Therefore, each respective coefficient must also be equal.

$$
\begin{align}
c_{1}&=c_{0} \\
2c_{2}&=c_{1}=c_{0}&&\implies c_{2}=\frac{1}{2} \\
3c_{3}&=c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}c_{0}&&\implies c_{3}=\frac{1}{2\cdot 3}c_{0} \\
&\dots&&\dots \\
n\cdot c_{n}&=c_{n-1}=\frac{c_{0}}{(n-1)!}&&\implies c_{n}=\frac{c_{0}}{n!}
\end{align}
$$
Recall that the coefficient for the [[#^ef8304]] is equal to $c_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}$, so we can say that the solution must be

$$
y=e^{x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{x^{n}}{n!}}
$$

We can solve more complex differential equations in a similar way - by representing both $y$ and $y'$ as Power Series and solving for $c_{n}$.
