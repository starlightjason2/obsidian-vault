# 2.2 - Special Matrices

## Hermitian Matrices

> [!def] Hermitian Matrix
> Any [[2.2 - Matrices#^5bf009]] where $\mathbf{A}$ is equal to the [[2.2 - Matrices#^hermitian-adjoint]] $\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}$.
> $$\mathbf{A}\text{ is Hermitian}\iff \mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}$$

^hermitian-matrix

> [!thm] Eigenvalues of Hermitian are Real
> Each [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvalue]] of a [[#^hermitian-matrix]] is a real number. Let $\ket{a}$ be the corresponding [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvector]].
> $$\hat{A}\ket{a} =a\ket{a}\implies a\in \mathbb{R}$$

^eigenvalues-of-hermitian-are-real

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^eigenvalues-of-hermitian-are-real]]
Take the [[2.2 - Matrices#^hermitian-adjoint]] of both sides of the eigenvalue/eigenvector equation.

$$
\begin{align}
\hat{A}\ket{a}&=a\ket{a} \implies\bra{\bar{a}} \hat{A}^{\dagger} =a\bra{\bar{a}}
\end{align}
$$

By definition, if $\hat{A}$ is a Hermitian, then $\hat{A}=\hat{A}^{\dagger}$, so $\bra{\bar{a}} \hat{A} =a\bra{\bar{a}}$.

$$
\begin{align}

\bra{a}\hat{A}\ket{a}=\bra{a}a\ket{a}   \implies
\bra{a}\hat{A}^{\dagger}\ket{a}=a\braket{a|a}
\end{align}
$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!thm] Eigenvectors of Hermitian are Orthogonal
> The set of each [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvector]] of a [[#^hermitian-matrix]] forms a complete [[2.1 - Vectors#^orthogonal-vectors]].
> $\hat{A}\ket{a} =a\ket{a}\implies \{\ket{a} \}\text{ is a complete orthogonal set}$

^eigenvectors-of-hermitian-are-orthogonal

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^eigenvectors-of-hermitian-are-orthogonal]]
We know that [[#^eigenvalues-of-hermitian-are-real]], and so $\bar{a}=a$. Taking the [[2.2 - Matrices#^hermitian-adjoint]] of both sides of the eigenvalue equation,

$$
\begin{align}
\hat{A}\ket{a_{1}}&=a_{1}\ket{a_{1}}\implies \bra{a_{1}} \hat{A}=\bra{a_{1}}a_{1} \\
\hat{A}\ket{a_{2}}&=a_{2}\ket{a_{2}}\implies \bra{a_{2}} \hat{A}=\bra{a_{2}}a_{2} \\[1em]

\bra{a_{1}}\hat{A}\ket{a_{2}}&=\bra{a_{1}}a_{2}\ket{a_{2}}=a_{1} \braket{a_{1}|a_{2}}\\
\bra{a_{2}}\hat{A}\ket{a_{1}}&=\bra{a_{2}}a_{1}\ket{a_{1}}=a_{2} \braket{a_{2}|a_{1}}=a_{2} \braket{a_{1}|a_{2}}
\end{align}
$$

So $a_{1}\neq a_{2}\implies \braket{a_{1}|a_{2}}=0$.
`\end{proof}`

## Shift Matrices

> [!def] Cyclic Up-Shift Matrix
> The **cyclic up-shift** $\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow}$ is the $n\times n$ [[2.2 - Matrices#^5bf009]] that sends each component of a vector one slot up, wrapping the last component back to the first. Acting on $(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})^{T}$ it returns $(b_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1})^{T}$.
> $$\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow}=\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

^shift-up-matrix

> [!def] Cyclic Down-Shift Matrix
> The **cyclic down-shift** $\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}$ is the [[2.2 - Matrices#^653a35|Transpose]] of the [[#^shift-up-matrix]], shifting each component one slot down and wrapping the first component to the last. It is also the inverse, $\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}=(\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow})^{-1}$.
> $$\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}=\begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \ddots & & \vdots \\ \vdots & & \ddots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

^shift-down-matrix

> [!thm] Spectrum of the Cyclic Shifts
> The [[#^shift-up-matrix]] and [[#^shift-down-matrix]] [[#^commuting-matrices|commute]], $[\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow},\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}]=0$, so they share [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvector|Eigenvectors]] though their [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvalue|Eigenvalues]] may differ. Indexing by $l=0,1,\dots,n-1$, the shared eigenvectors and eigenvalues are
> $$\ket{\lambda_{l}}=\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ \lambda_{l} \\ \lambda_{l}^{2} \\ \vdots \\ \lambda_{l}^{n-1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{S}^{\uparrow}\ket{\lambda_{l}}=\lambda_{l}\ket{\lambda_{l}},\qquad \mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}\ket{\lambda_{l}}=\lambda_{l}^{-1}\ket{\lambda_{l}}$$
> where $\lambda_{l}=e^{2\pi i l/n}$ are the $n$th roots of unity.

^spectrum-of-cyclic-shifts

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^spectrum-of-cyclic-shifts]]
Since $(\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow})^{n}=(\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow})^{n}=\mathbf{I}$, applying the eigenvalue equation $\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow}\ket{\lambda}=\lambda\ket{\lambda}$ a total of $n$ times gives $\ket{\lambda}=\lambda^{n}\ket{\lambda}$, so $\lambda^{n}=1$ and $\lambda_{l}=e^{2\pi i l/n}$ for $l=0,1,\dots,n-1$.

Writing the eigenvector componentwise as $\ket{\lambda}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})^{T}$, the action of $\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow}$ shifts every entry one slot up,

$$\mathbf{S}^{\uparrow}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1} \\ b_{2} \\ \vdots \\ b_{n-1} \\ b_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{2} \\ b_{3} \\ \vdots \\ b_{n} \\ b_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}b_{1} \\ b_{2} \\ \vdots \\ b_{n-1} \\ b_{n}\end{pmatrix}$$

so $b_{j+1}=\lambda\,b_{j}$. Setting $b_{1}=1$ iterates to $b_{2}=\lambda,\ b_{3}=\lambda^{2},\ \dots,\ b_{n}=\lambda^{n-1}$, giving the eigenvector quoted above.

Acting with $\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}$ on the same vector shifts every entry one slot down, wrapping $b_{1}$ to the bottom,

$$\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ \lambda \\ \vdots \\ \lambda^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{n-1} \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda^{n-2}\end{pmatrix}=\lambda^{n-1}\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ \lambda \\ \vdots \\ \lambda^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}$$

Since $\lambda^{n}=1$, the eigenvalue $\lambda^{n-1}=\lambda^{-1}=e^{-2\pi i l/n}$, confirming $\mathbf{S}^{\downarrow}\ket{\lambda_{l}}=\lambda_{l}^{-1}\ket{\lambda_{l}}$.
`\end{proof}`

## Projection Matrices

> [!def] Projection Matrix
> An [[2.2 - Matrices#^5bf009]] $\mathbf{P}_{v}$ that gives the same result when acting on any vector twice in succession as when it acts just once.
> $$(\mathbf{P})^{2}\equiv\mathbf{P}$$
>

^projection-matrix

## Commuting Matrices

> [!def] Commutator
> Let $A, \ \hat{B}$ each be a [[2.8 - Linear Operators#^linear-operator]]. The **commutator** $[\hat{A},\hat{B}]$ measures the difference between applying each in different order.
> $$[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]\equiv\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$$

^commutator

> [!def] Commuting Matrices
> **Commuting matrices** $\mathbf{A}, \ \mathbf{B}$ have a [[#^commutator]] of $[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]=0$.

^commuting-matrices

> [!thm] Commuting Matrices share Eigenvectors
> Each [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^basic-eigenvector]] with a Non-[[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^degenerate-eigenvalue]] of [[#^commuting-matrices]] $\mathbf{A}, \ \mathbf{B}$ is the same between $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.
> $$[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]=0\implies \begin{align}
\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}&=\alpha\,\mathbf{x} \\
\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{x}&=\beta\,\mathbf{x}
\end{align} \ \ \text{ for unique } \alpha, \ \beta$$

^f5881e

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^f5881e]]
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{v}&=\lambda\,\mathbf{v} \\
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{v})&=\mathbf{N}(\,\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{v} )\\
\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{v}&=\lambda\,(\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{v})
\end{align}
$$
Therefore, $\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{v}$ is an [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvector]] of $\mathbf{M}$ with the same *unique* eigenvalue $\lambda$. Therefore, $\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{v}\propto \mathbf{v}$, so $\mathbf{v}$ is also an eigenvector of $\mathbf{N}$.

`\end{proof}`
