# 3.7.1 - Sinusoidals

> [!def] Sinusoidal Function
> A sinusoidal $f(t)$ is a [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^periodic-function|Periodic Function]] in the form of a [[#^sin|Sine]] or [[#^cos|Cosine]] wave for amplitude $A$, angular frequency $\omega=2\pi/T$, and phase shift $\phi$. For $B_{1}=A\cos\phi, \ B_{2}=-A\sin\phi$, we can also express $f(t)$ as a sum of $\sin$ and $\cos$.
>
> $$f(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)=B_{1}\cos\omega t+B_{2}\sin\omega t$$
>
> Using [[3.6 - Complex Numbers#^eulers-formula]] and the [[3.6 - Complex Numbers#^complex-conjugate]] pair $C=Ae^{i\phi}/2, \ \bar{C}=Ae^{-i\phi}/2$, we can also express $f(t)$ in exponential form, or as the real part of a [[3.6 - Complex Numbers#^complex-number]] $D=Ae^{i\phi}$.
>
> $$f(t)=Ce^{i\omega t}+\bar{C}e^{-i\omega t}=\mathrm{Re}\Big[De^{i\omega t}\Big]$$

^sinusoidal-function

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^sinusoidal-function]]
Using the even and odd properties of sine and cosine,

$$
\begin{align}
x(t)=c_{1}\,e^{i\omega t}+c_{2}\,e^{-i\omega t}&=c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\cos(-\omega t)+i(c_{1}\sin(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(-\omega t)) \\[0.5em]
&=(c_{1}+c_{2})\cos\omega t+i(c_{1}-c_{2})\sin\omega t
\end{align}
$$

In order for $x(t)$ to be real, the conjugate of $c_{1}=C=\alpha+\beta i$ must be $c_{2}=\bar{C}=\alpha-\beta i$ so that $c_{1}+c_{2}=2\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$ and $i(c_{1}-c_{2})=-2i^{2}\beta=2\beta$.

$$x(t)=2\alpha\cos\omega t+2\beta\sin\omega t$$

Then we can also write $C=D\,e^{i\phi}, \ \bar{C}=D\,e^{-i\phi}$, so $x(t)=D(e^{i\phi}e^{\omega t}+e^{-i\phi}e^{-\omega t})$ and

$$x(t)=D(e^{i(\omega t+\phi)}+e^{-i(\omega t+\phi)})$$

With the identity $\cos\theta=(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})/2$, for $D=A/2$,

$$x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$$
`\end{proof}`

## Trigonometric

> [!def] Sin
> The sine of an angle $\theta$, written $\sin\theta$, gives the $y$-coordinate of the point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$ measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis.
>
> Equivalently, the odd part of the [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^complex-exponential|Complex Exponential]] $e^{i\theta}$, picked out as the imaginary part.
> $$\sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$$

^cef9d0

> [!def] Cos
> The cosine of an angle $\theta$, written $\cos\theta$, gives the $x$-coordinate of the point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$ measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis.
>
> Equivalently, the even part of the [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^complex-exponential|Complex Exponential]] $e^{i\theta}$, picked out as the real part.
> $$\cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}$$

^d2a1f2

## Hyperbolic

> [!def] Hyperbolic Cosine
> The **hyperbolic cosine** $\cosh x$ is the even part of the [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^complex-exponential|Complex Exponential]] on the real line. It plays the role of [[#^d2a1f2]] for hyperbolic geometry, parametrising the unit hyperbola $x^{2}-y^{2}=1$, and gives the shape of a hanging chain.
> $$\cosh x=\frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{2}$$
>
> Substituting $x\to i\theta$ links it to the ordinary cosine: $\cosh(i\theta)=\cos\theta$.

^hyperbolic-cosine

> [!def] Hyperbolic Sine
> The **hyperbolic sine** $\sinh x$ is the odd part of the [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^complex-exponential|Complex Exponential]] on the real line. It is to [[#^cef9d0]] what $\cosh$ is to [[#^d2a1f2|Cos]], and grows exponentially for large $|x|$.
> $$\sinh x=\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{2}$$
>
> Substituting $x\to i\theta$ relates it to the ordinary sine through a factor of $i$: $\sinh(i\theta)=i\sin\theta$.

^hyperbolic-sine

> [!thm] Hyperbolic Pythagorean Identity
> The [[#^cosh|Hyperbolic Cosine]] and [[#^sinh|Hyperbolic Sine]] satisfy a sign-flipped analogue of $\cos^{2}+\sin^{2}=1$, since the unit hyperbola differs from the unit circle by the same sign.
> $$\cosh^{2}x-\sinh^{2}x=1$$

^hyperbolic-pythagorean-identity
