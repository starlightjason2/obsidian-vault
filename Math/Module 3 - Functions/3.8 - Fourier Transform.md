# 3.8 - Fourier Transform

> [!def] Fourier Transform
> The Fourier Transform $\mathcal{F}$ is a [[2.7 - Function Spaces#^linear-operator]] $\mathcal{F}:f(t)\to F(\omega)$ representing any [[3.4 - Limits#^continuous-function]] as a combination of [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^periodic-function]] in the [[3.4 - Limits#^limit]] $\lim_{T\to\infty}$.
>
> $\mathcal{F}\left\{f(x) \right\}=F(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\,f(t)\,dt$

^fourier-transform

> [!def] Inverse Fourier Transform
> The [[2.5 - Linear Transformations#^585247]] of the [[#^fourier-transform]] is $\mathcal{F}^{-1}:F(\omega)\to f(t)$ where
> $$f(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega$$

^inverse-fourier-transform

> [!thm]
> Using the [[#^fourier-transform]], any [[3.1 - Scalar Functions#^function]] $f(t)$ can be expressed as a frequency-dependent [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^periodic-function]] $F(\omega)$ in the [[3.4 - Limits#^limit]] $\lim_{T\to\infty}$.

Consider the [[6.3 - Fourier Series#^fourier-series]] for $f(t)$ in the [[3.4 - Limits#^limit]] where the period $T\to\infty$. The frequency spacing $\Delta\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}\to d\omega$ and $\frac{1}{T}\to \frac{d\omega}{2\pi}$.

$$f(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\left( \frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{-in\omega t}\,dt \right)e^{in\omega t}}$$

As $T\to\infty$, $n\omega\to \omega$ and the [[3.9 - Integrals#^riemann-sum]] $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\to \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,d\omega$.

$$
f(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,d\omega \,e^{i\omega t}\left[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt \right]
$$

Or, the [[#^inverse-fourier-transform]] of the [[#^fourier-transform]] is the function $f(t)$.

> [!thm] Fourier Transform of the Derivative
> Given the [[#^fourier-transform]] $F(\omega)\leftrightarrow f(x)$, the transform of the [[3.5 - Derivatives#^derivative]] $f'(x)$ is,
> $$\mathcal{F}\left\{   \frac{df}{dx}   \right\}=i\omega F(\omega)$$
>

^fourier-transform-of-the-derivative

## Common Transforms

> [!example] Fourier Transform of Dirac Delta
> The [[#^fourier-transform]] of the [[3.5 - Step and Delta#^017ca2]] $\delta(t-t_0)$ is a constant.
>
> From the integral $\mathcal{F}\Big \{\delta(t-t_{0})\Big \}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-t_0)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-i\omega t_0}$.
>
> $$f(t)=\delta(t-t_{0})\quad \leftrightarrow\quad F(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-i\omega t_0}$$

^fourier-transform-of-dirac-delta

> [!example] Fourier Transform of Gaussian
> The [[#^fourier-transform]] of a Gaussian is also a Gaussian with a new $\sigma'=1 / \sigma$.
> $$f(t)=e^{-\frac{t^2}{2\sigma^2}}\quad \leftrightarrow\quad F(\omega)=\sigma e^{-\frac{\sigma^2\omega^2}{2}}$$

^fourier-transform-of-gaussian

> [!example] Exponential Decay
> The [[#^fourier-transform]] of an exponential decay gives a Lorentzian.
> $$f(t)=e^{-\alpha|t|}\quad \leftrightarrow\quad F(\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^2+\omega^2}$$

> [!example] Rectangular Pulse
> The [[#^fourier-transform]] of a rectangular pulse.
> $$f(t)=\begin{cases}1 & |t|<a \\ 0 & |t|>a\end{cases}\quad \leftrightarrow\quad F(\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sin(a\omega)}{\omega}$$

> [!example] Constant Function
> The [[#^fourier-transform]] of a constant $f(t)=1$ is a [[3.5 - Step and Delta#^017ca2]] in frequency space.
> $$f(t)=1\quad \leftrightarrow\quad F(\omega)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\delta(\omega)$$

> [!example] Fourier Transform of $\sin(ax)$
> Using the Euler identity $\sin(ax)=\frac{1}{2i}(e^{iax}-e^{-iax})$ and $\mathcal{F}\{e^{iax}\}=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\delta(k-a)$,
> $$\mathcal{F}\{\sin(ax)\}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2i}\left[\delta(k-a)-\delta(k+a)\right]=-i\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left[\delta(k-a)-\delta(k+a)\right]$$
>
> In quantum mechanics, $\sin(kx)$ is a superposition of two plane waves $e^{\pm ikx}$ — directly visible from the two delta functions.

^ftsin

> [!example] Fourier Transform of $\cos(ax)$
> Using the Euler identity $\cos(ax)=\frac{1}{2}(e^{iax}+e^{-iax})$,
> $$\mathcal{F}\{\cos(ax)\}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left[\delta(k-a)+\delta(k+a)\right]$$
>
> Consistent with the [[#^fourier-transform-of-the-derivative]]: $\mathcal{F}\!\left\{\frac{d}{dx}\sin(ax)\right\}=ik\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\sin(ax)\}$, which recovers $a\cdot\mathcal{F}\{\cos(ax)\}$.

^fourier-transform-of-cosax

## FFT

> [!def] Fast Fourier Transform
> Given a [[#^fourier-transform]] $f(t)\leftrightarrow F(\omega)$, the discrete Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is,
> $$F(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2T}}\left( \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{f_{k}e^{-i\omega t}} \right)\Delta t$$
