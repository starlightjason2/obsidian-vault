# 3.7 - Multiple Derivatives

> [!def] $n$th Derivative
> Given a function $f(x)$, the $n$th [[3.5 - Derivatives#^derivative]] $f^{(n)}$ is the first derivative applied $n$ times to $f$.
>
> $$\frac{d^{n}f}{dx^{n}} = \frac{df^{(n-1)}}{dx} \quad \text{for } n > 1$$
>
> For consistency, the $0$th derivative is just $f$.

^nth-derivative

> [!def] $n$th Partial Derivative
> Given a function $f(x_{1}, \, \dots, \, x_{n})$, the $n$th [[3.5 - Derivatives#^partial-derivative]] is the partial derivative applied in the order $\alpha=(\alpha_{1}, \, \dots, \, a_{n})$ for each variable $(x_{1}, \, \dots, \, x_{n})$. Also indicated with the differential operator $\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}$.
>
> $$\frac{\partial^{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert} f}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\,\dots \,\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}$$
>
> For consistency, the $0$th derivative is just $f$.

^nth-partial-derivative



## Second Derivatives

> [!def] Second Derivative
> Given a function $f(x)$, the second derivative is the [[3.5 - Derivatives#^derivative]] applied twice.
> $$\frac{d^{2}f}{dx^{2}} = \frac{d}{dx}\Big[\frac{df}{dx}\Big]$$

^second-derivative

Partial derivatives can have nested partial derivatives. Notice that walking 2 steps in $x$ and then 3 steps in $y$ will result in the same **change in elevation** as walking 3 steps in $y$ and then 2 steps in $x$. This is formalized in Clairaut's Theorem.

> [!def] Second Partial Derivative
> Given a function $f(\mathbf{x})$, the second derivative $f_{x_{n}x_{m}}$ is the [[3.5 - Derivatives#^partial-derivative]] first applied with respect $x_{n}$ and then with respect to $x_{m}$.
> $$f_{x_{n}x_{m}}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_{n} \partial x_{m}}$$

^second-partial-derivative

> [!thm] Clairaut's Theorem
> Given a function $f$, if every [[#^second-partial-derivative]] is a [[3.4 - Limits#^continuous-function]] then the order of differentiation doesn't matter.
>
> $$\frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial y\,\partial x}=\frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial x\,\partial y}$$

^clairauts-theorem

### Second Derivative Test

> [!def] Critical Points
> A **critical point** of $f$ is an input point $\mathbf{x}$ where the [[3.5 - Derivatives#^5bb63d]] $\nabla f=0$ or any [[3.5 - Derivatives#^partial-derivative]] not exist. In three dimensions, these points will be either minima, maxima, or saddle points.
> $$\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}$$

^critical-points

> [!thm] Second Derivative Test
> Let $P=(x_{0},y_{0})$ be one of the [[#^critical-points]] of $f(x,y)$. Define the **discriminant** as
>
> $$D=f_{xx}(x_{0},y_{0})f_{yy}(x_{0},y_{0})-f_{xy}(x_{0},y_{0})^{2}$$
>
> 1. If $D>0$ and $fxx(x_{0},y_{0})>0$, then $P$ is a **local minimum**.
> 2. If $D>0$ and $fxx(x_{0},y_{0})<0$, then $P$ is a **local maximum**.
> 3. if $D<0$, then $P$ is a **saddle point**.
> 4. If $D=0$, nothing can be concluded.
>

^second-derivative-test

### Constrained Optimization

When gradients are parallel, the only way to change the output is by leaving the constraint, so that point must be the extrema.

> [!thm] Constrained Optimization with Lagrange Multipliers
> Let there be a function $f(\mathbf{x})$ that we want to **optimize**. Let there be $g_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,g_{k-1}(\mathbf{x})$ **constraint functions**.
>
> The [[3.5 - Derivatives#^5bb63d]] $(x,y)=z$ will be parallel at every minimum or maximum point $(x_{0},y_{0})$ on a constraint $g(x,y)=0$.
>
> $$\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\nabla g_{1}} & \dots & \mathbf{\nabla g_{n}}
\end{bmatrix}\,\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_{1} \\
\vdots \\
\lambda_{n}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial g_{1}}{\partial x_{1}} & \dots & \frac{\partial g_{n}}{\partial x_{n}} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
\frac{\partial g_{1}}{\partial x_{n}} & \dots & \frac{\partial g_{n}}{\partial x_{n}}
\end{bmatrix}\,\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_{1} \\
\vdots \\
\lambda_{n}
\end{bmatrix}$$

## Multiple Vector Derivatives

> [!def] Laplacian
> The **Laplacian** of a multivariable function $f(\mathbf{x})$ is the [[3.6 - Vector Derivatives#^divergence]] of the [[3.5 - Derivatives#^5bb63d]] of $f$.
>
> In any [[2.6 - Coordinate Sytems#^9cf7bf]] $(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})$,
> $$\nabla^2 f = \frac{1}{h_1 h_2 h_3} \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial q_1} \left( \frac{h_2 h_3}{h_1} \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_1} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial q_2} \left( \frac{h_3 h_1}{h_2} \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_2} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial q_3} \left( \frac{h_1 h_2}{h_3} \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_3} \right)
\right]$$
> In [[2.6 - Coordinate Sytems#^cartesian-coordinates]] this simplifies to
> $$\nabla ^{2}f=\nabla \cdot \nabla f$$
>
