# 4.1.5 - First Order Systems

> [!def] First Order System
> Given a [[3.2 - Vector Functions#^c8839e|Vector Function]] $\mathbf{x}(t)\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, a linear system of ODEs is a collection of $n$ coupled [[4.1.1 - First Order Linear ODEs#^655517|First Order Linear ODEs]]:
> $$\begin{align}
x_{1}'&=a_{11}(t)\,x_{1}+\dots+a_{1n}(t)\,x_{n}+f_{1}(t) \\
&\vdots \\
x_{n}'&=a_{n1}(t)\,x_{1}+\dots+a_{nn}(t)\,x_{n}+f_{n}(t)
\end{align}$$
> Encoding the coefficients in a [[2.2 - Matrices#^5bf009|Matrix]] $\mathbf{A}(t)$ and the forcing terms in a vector $\mathbf{f}(t)$, the system collapses into a single matrix equation:
> $$\frac{d{\mathbf{x}}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}(t)\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}(t)$$

^sysdef

> [!def] Homogeneous Linear System of ODEs
> A [[#^sysdef]] where each equation is [[4.1.0 - Linear ODEs#^a5e477|Homogeneous]].
> $$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}(t)\,\mathbf{x}$$

^homsys

> [!def] Constant Coefficient System
> A [[#^sysdef|Linear System of ODEs]] in which each equation is a [[4.1.3 - Constant Coefficient ODEs^]] $\mathbf{A}(t)\equiv \mathbf{A}$ is a [[2.2 - Matrices#^5bf009|Matrix]] with constant entries.
> $$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}(t)$$

^constsys

## Fundamental Solutions

> [!def] Fundamental Matrix
> Let $\{\mathbf{x}_{1}(t),\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}(t)\}$ be [[4.1.0 - Linear ODEs#^9d5987|Fundamental Solutions]] of a [[#^homsys|Homogeneous Linear System]]. The **fundamental [[2.2 - Matrices#^5bf009]]** $\mathbf{\Phi}(t)$ has each fundamental solution as a column.
> $$\mathbf{\Phi}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}(t) & \dots & \mathbf{x}_{n}(t)\end{bmatrix}$$
> The general homogeneous solution is $\mathbf{x}_{h}(t)=\mathbf{\Phi}(t)\,\mathbf{c}$ for an arbitrary constant vector $\mathbf{c}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$.

^fundmat

> [!thm] System Wronskian
> The [[4.1.0 - Linear ODEs#^adbb90|Wronskian]] of a system is the [[2.4 - Determinants#^add7e3|Determinant]] of the [[#^fundmat|Fundamental Matrix]]. The solutions are linearly independent IFF $W(t)\neq 0$.
> $$W(t)=\det \mathbf{\Phi}(t)$$

^syswronskian

## Eigenvalue Solution Method

> [!thm] Eigenvalue Ansatz
> Consider a [[#^constsys|Constant Coefficient]] [[#^homsys|Homogeneous System]] $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$. Substituting the ansatz $\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$ reduces the system to the [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^b325d4|Eigenvalue]] equation:
> $$\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{v}=\lambda\,\mathbf{v}$$
> Each [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^888169|Eigenvalue]] $\lambda_{i}$ with [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^b325d4|Eigenvector]] $\mathbf{v}_{i}$ produces a fundamental solution $\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)=e^{\lambda_{i} t}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}$.

^eigans

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^eigans]]
Differentiating $\mathbf{x}=e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$ gives $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\lambda\,e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$. Substituting into $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ and dividing through by $e^{\lambda t}\neq 0$ yields $\lambda\,\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{v}$, the [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^b325d4|Eigenvector]] equation. The [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^223c29|Characteristic Polynomial]] $\det(\lambda \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})=0$ provides the eigenvalues; each [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^8c43f5|Eigenspace]] supplies the corresponding eigenvectors.
`\end{proof}`

> [!thm] General Solution (Diagonalizable Case)
> Let $\mathbf{A}$ be a [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^6089b9|Diagonalizable Matrix]] with [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^b325d4|Eigenvectors]] $\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}\}$ spanning $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and corresponding [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^888169|Eigenvalues]] $\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}$. The general solution to $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ is a superposition over the [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^1a2381|Eigenbasis]]:
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\,e^{\lambda_{i} t}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}$$
> Equivalently, in matrix form with $\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{1} & \dots & \mathbf{v}_{n}\end{bmatrix}$ and diagonal $\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})$:
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{P}\,e^{\mathbf{D} t}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}(0)$$

^gensol-diag

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^gensol-diag]]
By the [[#^eigans|Eigenvalue Ansatz]], each $(\lambda_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i})$ pair contributes a solution $e^{\lambda_{i} t}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}$. Linear independence of $\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}$ guarantees these are [[4.1.0 - Linear ODEs#^9d5987|Fundamental Solutions]], so any linear combination is the general solution.

For the matrix form, use [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^4fddab|Diagonalization]] $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}$. Since $\mathbf{P}$ is constant, the change of variables $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}$ gives $\dot{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{y}$, decoupling the system into $n$ independent scalar equations $\dot{y}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\,y_{i}$ with solutions $y_{i}(t)=e^{\lambda_{i}t}\,y_{i}(0)$. Returning to the original basis: $\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{P}\,e^{\mathbf{D} t}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}(0)$.
`\end{proof}`

### Complex Eigenvalues

> [!thm] Complex Eigenvalue Solutions
> If a real matrix $\mathbf{A}$ has a complex [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^888169|Eigenvalue]] $\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$ with [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^b325d4|Eigenvector]] $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{a}+i\mathbf{b}$, then $\bar\lambda=\alpha-i\beta$ is also an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\bar{\mathbf{v}}$. Taking real and imaginary parts of $e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$ yields two real fundamental solutions:
> $$\begin{align}
\mathbf{x}_{1}(t)&=e^{\alpha t}\big(\mathbf{a}\cos\beta t-\mathbf{b}\sin\beta t\big) \\[0.5em]
\mathbf{x}_{2}(t)&=e^{\alpha t}\big(\mathbf{a}\sin\beta t+\mathbf{b}\cos\beta t\big)
\end{align}$$
> The trajectories spiral outward for $\alpha>0$, inward for $\alpha<0$, and form closed orbits for $\alpha=0$.

^complex-eig

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^complex-eig]]
By [[3.6 - Complex Numbers|Euler's formula]], $e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}=e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t+i\sin\beta t)(\mathbf{a}+i\mathbf{b})=e^{\alpha t}\big[(\mathbf{a}\cos\beta t-\mathbf{b}\sin\beta t)+i(\mathbf{a}\sin\beta t+\mathbf{b}\cos\beta t)\big]$. Since $\mathbf{A}$ is real, the real and imaginary parts each satisfy $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ separately.
`\end{proof}`

### Repeated Eigenvalues

> [!thm] Generalized Eigenvector Solution
> Suppose $\lambda$ is an [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^888169|Eigenvalue]] with [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^dbcea1|Algebraic Multiplicity]] $k$ but [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^323406|Geometric Multiplicity]] less than $k$. The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^6089b9|defective]], and the missing fundamental solutions are obtained from **generalized eigenvectors** $\mathbf{w}_{j}$ satisfying
> $$(\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I})^{j}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}=\mathbf{0},\qquad (\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I})^{j-1}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}\neq \mathbf{0}$$
> Each $\mathbf{w}_{j}$ contributes a fundamental solution
> $$\mathbf{x}_{j}(t)=e^{\lambda t}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\,(\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I})^{i}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}$$

^geneig

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^geneig]]
Let $\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I}$. Since $\mathbf{A}$ and $\lambda \mathbf{I}$ commute, the [[2.2 - Matrices#^8805f7|Matrix Exponential]] factors as $e^{\mathbf{A} t}=e^{\lambda t}\,e^{\mathbf{N} t}$. The generalized eigenvector condition $\mathbf{N}^{j}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}=\mathbf{0}$ truncates the Taylor series for $e^{\mathbf{N} t}$:
$$e^{\mathbf{N} t}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\,\mathbf{N}^{i}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}$$
Multiplying by $e^{\lambda t}$ gives the stated solution. The $j=1$ case recovers the ordinary [[#^eigans|Eigenvalue Ansatz]].
`\end{proof}`

## Matrix Exponential Solution

> [!thm] Matrix Exponential Solution
> The unique solution to the [[#^constsys|Constant Coefficient]] [[#^homsys|Homogeneous System]] $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ with initial condition $\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}$ is:
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\mathbf{A} t}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}$$
> The [[2.2 - Matrices#^8805f7|Matrix Exponential]] $e^{\mathbf{A} t}$ is itself the [[#^fundmat|Fundamental Matrix]] satisfying $\mathbf{\Phi}(0)=\mathbf{I}$.

^matexp-sol

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^matexp-sol]]
Differentiating the [[2.2 - Matrices#^8805f7|Matrix Exponential]] series term by term:
$$\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf{A} t}=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{A} t)^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbf{A}^{n}\,t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\mathbf{A}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{A} t)^{m}}{m!}=\mathbf{A}\,e^{\mathbf{A} t}$$
So $\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\mathbf{A} t}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}$ satisfies $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$, and at $t=0$ the series gives $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0}=\mathbf{I}$, so $\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}$. Uniqueness follows from the [[4.1.0 - Linear ODEs#^02185f|Linear IVP Theorem]].
`\end{proof}`

> [!cor] Matrix Exponential via Diagonalization
> When $\mathbf{A}$ is [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^6089b9|Diagonalizable]] as $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}$, the [[2.2 - Matrices#^8805f7|Matrix Exponential]] reduces to scalar exponentials of the eigenvalues:
> $$e^{\mathbf{A} t}=\mathbf{P}\,e^{\mathbf{D} t}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\mathbf{P}\begin{bmatrix}e^{\lambda_{1}t} & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & e^{\lambda_{n}t}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}^{-1}$$
> Substituting into [[#^matexp-sol]] recovers the eigenbasis expansion of [[#^gensol-diag]].

^matexp-diag

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^matexp-diag]]
Powers of a [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^4fddab|Diagonalized Matrix]] satisfy $\mathbf{A}^{n}=\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{D}^{n}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}$, since the inner $\mathbf{P}^{-1}\,\mathbf{P}$ pairs cancel. Substituting into the [[2.2 - Matrices#^8805f7|Matrix Exponential]] series:
$$e^{\mathbf{A} t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{A} t)^{n}}{n!}=\mathbf{P}\!\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{D} t)^{n}}{n!}\right)\!\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\mathbf{P}\,e^{\mathbf{D} t}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}$$
The exponential of a diagonal matrix acts entry-wise, giving $(e^{\mathbf{D} t})_{ii}=e^{\lambda_{i}t}$.
`\end{proof}`

## Inhomogeneous Systems

> [!thm] Variation of Parameters for Systems
> The general solution to the [[#^sysdef|Linear System]] $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}(t)\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}(t)$, given a [[#^fundmat|Fundamental Matrix]] $\mathbf{\Phi}(t)$ for the homogeneous system, is:
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{\Phi}(t)\,\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{\Phi}(t)\int \mathbf{\Phi}^{-1}(t)\,\mathbf{f}(t)\,dt$$
> For a [[#^constsys|Constant Coefficient System]] with initial condition $\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}$, this becomes:
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\mathbf{A} t}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathbf{A}(t-s)}\,\mathbf{f}(s)\,ds$$

^varparam-sys

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^varparam-sys]]
Try the ansatz $\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{\Phi}(t)\,\mathbf{u}(t)$. Differentiating, $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\dot{\mathbf{\Phi}}\,\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{\Phi}\,\dot{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{\Phi}\,\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{\Phi}\,\dot{\mathbf{u}}$, since columns of $\mathbf{\Phi}$ solve the homogeneous system. Substituting into $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}$ gives $\mathbf{\Phi}\,\dot{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{f}$, so $\dot{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{-1}\,\mathbf{f}$. Integrating gives the stated form.

For the constant coefficient case, use $\mathbf{\Phi}(t)=e^{\mathbf{A} t}$ with $\mathbf{\Phi}^{-1}(t)=e^{-\mathbf{A} t}$, and $e^{\mathbf{A} t}\,e^{-\mathbf{A} s}=e^{\mathbf{A}(t-s)}$.
`\end{proof}`
