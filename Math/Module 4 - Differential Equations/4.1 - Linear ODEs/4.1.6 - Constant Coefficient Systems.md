# 4.1.6 - Constant Coefficient Systems

> [!def] Constant Coefficient System
> A [[4.1.5 - First Order Systems#^sysdef|Linear System of ODEs]] where $\mathbf{A}(t)\equiv \mathbf{A}$ is a [[2.2 - Matrices#^5bf009|Matrix]] with constant entries.
> $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}(t)$

^constant-coefficient-system

## Eigenvector Solution

> [!thm] Eigenvector Solution of a Constant Coefficient System
> Consider a [[#^constant-coefficient-system|Constant Coefficient]] [[4.1.5 - First Order Systems#^homogeneous-linear-system-of-odes|Homogeneous System]] $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$. Substituting $\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$ reduces the system to an eigenvalue equation.
>
>
> $$\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{v}=\lambda\,\mathbf{v}$$
>
> Therefore, the general solution to ${d\mathbf{x}}/{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ is a superposition over the [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eigenbasis|Eigenbasis]] formed by the [[4.1.5 - First Order Systems#^fundamental-matrix]] of $\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)=e^{\lambda_{i} t}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}$.
>

^eigenvector-solution-of-a-constant-coefficient-system

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^eigenvector-solution-of-a-constant-coefficient-system]]
Differentiating $\mathbf{x}=e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$ gives $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\lambda\,e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$. Substituting into $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ and dividing through by $e^{\lambda t}\neq 0$ yields $\lambda\,\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{v}$, the [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvector|Eigenvector]] equation. The [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^characteristic-polynomial|Characteristic Polynomial]] $\det(\lambda \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})=0$ provides the eigenvalues; each [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eigenspace|Eigenspace]] supplies the corresponding eigenvectors.
`\end{proof}`

> [!cor] General Solution of Diagonalizable System
> Let $\mathbf{A}$ be a [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^6089b9|Diagonalizable Matrix]] with eigenvectors $\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{n}\}$ spanning $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and corresponding eigenvalues $\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}$. Use the [[#^eigenvector-solution-of-a-constant-coefficient-system]],
>
> The general solution to $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ is a superposition over the [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eigenbasis|Eigenbasis]]:
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\,e^{\lambda_{i} t}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}$$
> Equivalently, in matrix form with $\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{1} & \dots & \mathbf{v}_{n}\end{bmatrix}$ and diagonal $\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})$:
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{P}\,e^{\mathbf{D} t}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}(0)$$

^general-solution-of-diagonalizable-system

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^general-solution-of-diagonalizable-system]]
By the [[#^eigenvector-solution-of-a-constant-coefficient-system|Eigenvalue Ansatz]], each $(\lambda_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i})$ pair contributes a solution $e^{\lambda_{i} t}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}$. Linear independence of $\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}$ guarantees these are [[4.1.0 - Linear ODEs#^fundamental-solutions|Fundamental Solutions]], so any linear combination is the general solution.

For the matrix form, use [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^4fddab|Diagonalization]] $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}$. Since $\mathbf{P}$ is constant, the change of variables $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}$ gives $\dot{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{y}$, decoupling the system into $n$ independent scalar equations $\dot{y}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\,y_{i}$ with solutions $y_{i}(t)=e^{\lambda_{i}t}\,y_{i}(0)$. Returning to the original basis: $\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{P}\,e^{\mathbf{D} t}\,\mathbf{P}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}(0)$.
`\end{proof}`

### Complex Eigenvalues

> [!thm] Complex Eigenvalue Solutions
> If a real matrix $\mathbf{A}$ has a complex [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvalue|Eigenvalue]] $\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$ with [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvector|Eigenvector]] $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{a}+i\mathbf{b}$, then $\bar\lambda=\alpha-i\beta$ is also an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\bar{\mathbf{v}}$. Taking real and imaginary parts of $e^{\lambda t}\,\mathbf{v}$ yields two real fundamental solutions:
> $$\begin{align}
\mathbf{x}_{1}(t)&=e^{\alpha t}\big(\mathbf{a}\cos\beta t-\mathbf{b}\sin\beta t\big) \\[0.5em]
\mathbf{x}_{2}(t)&=e^{\alpha t}\big(\mathbf{a}\sin\beta t+\mathbf{b}\cos\beta t\big)
\end{align}$$
> The trajectories spiral outward for $\alpha>0$, inward for $\alpha<0$, and form closed orbits for $\alpha=0$.

^complex-eig

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^complex-eig]]
By [[3.6 - Complex Numbers|Euler's formula]],

$$e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}=e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t+i\sin\beta t)(\mathbf{a}+i\mathbf{b})=e^{\alpha t}\big[(\mathbf{a}\cos\beta t-\mathbf{b}\sin\beta t)+i(\mathbf{a}\sin\beta t+\mathbf{b}\cos\beta t)\big]$$

Since $\mathbf{A}$ is real, the real and imaginary parts each satisfy $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ separately.
`\end{proof}`

### Repeated Eigenvalues

> [!thm] Generalized Eigenvector Solution
> Suppose $\lambda$ is an [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^eiegenvalue|Eigenvalue]] with [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^algebraic-multiplicity|Algebraic Multiplicity]] $k$ but [[2.6 - Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues#^geometric-multiplicity|Geometric Multiplicity]] less than $k$. The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is [[2.2 - Matrix Decomposition#^6089b9|defective]], and the missing fundamental solutions are obtained from **generalized eigenvectors** $\mathbf{w}_{j}$ satisfying
> $$(\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I})^{j}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}=\mathbf{0},\qquad (\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I})^{j-1}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}\neq \mathbf{0}$$
> Each $\mathbf{w}_{j}$ contributes a fundamental solution
> $$\mathbf{x}_{j}(t)=e^{\lambda t}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\,(\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I})^{i}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}$$

^generalized-eigenvector-solution

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^generalized-eigenvector-solution]]
Let $\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{A}-\lambda \mathbf{I}$. Since $\mathbf{A}$ and $\lambda \mathbf{I}$ commute, the [[2.2 - Matrices#^matrix-exponential|Matrix Exponential]] factors as $e^{\mathbf{A} t}=e^{\lambda t}\,e^{\mathbf{N} t}$. The generalized eigenvector condition $\mathbf{N}^{j}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}=\mathbf{0}$ truncates the Taylor series for $e^{\mathbf{N} t}$:
$$e^{\mathbf{N} t}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\,\mathbf{N}^{i}\,\mathbf{w}_{j}$$
Multiplying by $e^{\lambda t}$ gives the stated solution. The $j=1$ case recovers the ordinary [[#^eigenvector-solution-of-a-constant-coefficient-system|Eigenvalue Ansatz]].
`\end{proof}`

## Matrix Exponential Solution

> [!thm] Matrix Exponential Solution
> The unique solution to the [[#^constant-coefficient-system|Constant Coefficient]] [[4.1.5 - First Order Systems#^homogeneous-linear-system-of-odes|Homogeneous System]] $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$ with initial condition $\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}$ is the [[2.2 - Matrices#^matrix-exponential|Matrix Exponential]] $e^{\mathbf{A} t}$, which is itself the [[#^fundamental-matrix|Fundamental Matrix]] satisfying $\mathbf{\Phi}(0)=\mathbf{I}$.
> $$\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\mathbf{A} t}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}$$
>

^matrix-exponential-solution

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^matrix-exponential-solution]]
Differentiating the [[2.2 - Matrices#^matrix-exponential|Matrix Exponential]] series term by term:
$$\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mathbf{A} t}=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{A} t)^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbf{A}^{n}\,t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\mathbf{A}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mathbf{A} t)^{m}}{m!}=\mathbf{A}\,e^{\mathbf{A} t}$$
So $\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{\mathbf{A} t}\,\mathbf{x}_{0}$ satisfies $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}$, and at $t=0$ the series gives $e^{\mathbf{A}\cdot 0}=\mathbf{I}$, so $\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}$. Uniqueness follows from the [[4.1.0 - Linear ODEs#^02185f|Linear IVP Theorem]].
`\end{proof}`
