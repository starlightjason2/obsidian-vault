# 1.10 - Fluid Dynamics

Fluid flow can be represented with **vector fields**. Parallel flow lines indicate laminar flow. Non-parallel flow lines indicate turbulent flow.

## Flow Rate

> [!def] Volume Flow Rate
> **Volume flow rate** $Q\left[\pu{m^{3}/s}\right]$ is the volume $V$ of fluid flowing past a certain point per second. If $v$ is the velocity of the fluid and $A$ is the cross-sectional area of a cylinder of fluid, we can also say
>
> $$Q=\frac{dV}{dt}=Av$$

^73f604

> [!def] Mass Flow Rate
> **Mass flow rate** $Q\left[\pu{kg/s}\right]$ is the mass $m$ of fluid flowing past a certain point per second. Let $\rho$ be the density of the fluid and $A$ be the cross-sectional area of a cylinder of fluid.
>
> $$\frac{dm}{dt}=\rho Av$$

^7587a9

> [!thm] Equation of Continuity
> For an **incompressible fluid**, the mass and volume flow rate of a fluid will be the **same at all points**. If there are no sources or sinks in the fluid, the mass and volume flowing through one section ($A_{1}$) of the fluid must equal the mass and volume flowing through any other section $A_{2}$. So the [[#^7587a9]] and [[#^73f604]] remain **constant**.
>
> $$\rho_{1}A_{1}v_{1}=\rho_{2}A_{2}v_{2}$$

^7202c6

`\begin{proof}`[[#^7202c6]]
Consider a cylinder of liquid with length $x$ and cross-sectional area $A$. The velocity of the liquid is $v=\frac{dx}{dt}$ and the mass is $m=\rho V=\rho Ax$.

Then the volume flow rate is

$$
Q=\frac{dV}{dt}=A\frac{dx}{dt}=Av
$$

And the mass flow rate is

$$
\frac{dm}{dt}=\rho A\frac{dx}{dt}=\rho Av
$$

`\end{proof}`

## Bernoulli's Principle

Notice that a consequence of the [[#^7202c6]] is that fluid **speeds up** as it passes through a smaller area, and vice versa. This requires [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^e395c7]], which means a force must be applied to move the fluid. The conservation of energy gives us Bernoulli's Equation. The result, without a change in elevation, is that if the **velocity increases** then the **pressure must decrease**.

> [!thm] Bernoulli's Equation
> Given a fluid with constant density $\rho$, consider the velocity $v$ and pressure $p$ at any two points with an elevation $\Delta y$.
>
> $$p_{1}+\rho\,y_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\rho\,v_{1}^{2}=p_{2}+\rho\,y_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho\,v_{2}^{2}$$
>
> For fluid flow without a change in elevation ($y_{1}=y_{2}$),
>
$$\Delta p+\frac{1}{2}\rho(\Delta v)^{2}=0$$

^dc697d

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^dc697d]]

![[Pasted image 20250714171955.png]]

We can assume that the force remains parallel to the displacement, simplifying the calculus. Notice that the pressure on the other end of the pipe is in the opposite direction. Since this is the cross section of a larger pipe, there is always inward fluid pressure at all points.

$$
\begin{align}
\Delta W&=F_{1}\,\Delta x_{1}-F_{2}\,\Delta x_{2} \\
&=p_{1}A_{1}\Delta x_{1}-p_{2}A_{2}\Delta x_{2} \\
&=p_{1}\Delta V_{1}-p_{2}\Delta V_{2}
\end{align}
$$

Or, in the limit,

$$
dW=(p_{1}-p_{2})\,dV
$$

^174af4

The mass is $m=\rho\Delta V$, so the change in mechanical energy between point $1$ and $2$ is

$$
\begin{align}
\Delta PE&=\rho g\Delta V(y_{2}-y_{1}) \\
\Delta KE&=\frac{1}{2}\rho\Delta V(v_{2}^{2}-v_{1}^{2}) \\
\Delta ME&=\Delta PE+\Delta KE \\
&=\rho\Delta V\left( g(y_{2}-y_{1})+\frac{1}{2}(v_{2}^{2}-v_{1}^{2}) \right) \\
\end{align}
$$

And in the limit,

$$
dE=\rho \left( g(y_{2}-y_{1})+\frac{1}{2}(v_{2}^{2}-v_{1}^{2}) \right)\,dV \\
$$

^dbbc4d

We know that $dW=dE$, because energy is the ability to do work.

So substituting [[#^174af4]] and [[#^dbbc4d]], we get

$$
 (p_{1}-p_{2})\cancel{dV}=\rho \left( g(y_{2}-y_{1})+\frac{1}{2}(v_{2}^{2}-v_{1}^{2}) \right)\,\cancel{dV} \\
$$

$$p_{1}+\rho\,y_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\rho\,v_{1}^{2}=p_{2}+\rho\,y_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho\,v_{2}^{2}$$

`\end{proof}`
