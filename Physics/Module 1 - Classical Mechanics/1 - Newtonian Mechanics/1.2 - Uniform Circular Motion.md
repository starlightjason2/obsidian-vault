# 1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion

An object moving in a circle with constant speed $v\, [\pu{m/s}]$ and a period $T\, [\pu{s}]$ is experiencing **uniform circular motion**. We can see geometrically that the velocity vector is:

$$
\mathbf{v} = v\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\imath}} + v\cos\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}
$$

Furthermore, we see that for an angle $\theta \left[\pu{rad}\right]$ the arc length $\mathbf{s}\left[\pu{m}\right]$ traced by the particle must be

$$
\lVert{\mathbf{s}}\rVert =\lVert{\mathbf{R}}\rVert \lVert{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert
$$

and we define the direction of $\mathbf{s}$ to be orthogonal to both $\mathbf{\theta}$ and $\mathbf{R}$.

$$
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{R}\times \mathbf{\theta}
$$

## Angular Motion Variables

> [!def] Angular Displacement
> Angular displacement $\mathbf{\theta} \left[\pu{rad}\right]$ measures how far an object has rotated from a reference direction, typically $\hat{x}$.

^bc0269

> [!def] Angular Velocity
> Angular velocity $\omega$ ($\pu{rad/s}$) is the rate of change of [[#^bc0269]]. Furthermore, given a tangential velocity $v_{t}\left[\pu{m/s}\right]$ and a radius $r\left[\pu{m}\right]$,
>
> $$
> \omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}=\frac{v_{t}}{r}
> $$

^475e51

> [!def] Angular Acceleration
> Angular acceleration $\alpha$ ( $\pu{rad/s^2}$) is the rate of change of angular velocity. Furthermore, given a tangential acceleration $a_{t}\left[\pu{m/s^{2}}\right]$ and a radius $r\left[\pu{m}\right]$,
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{a_{t}}{r}
> $$

^fb8801

Angular motion has parallels to linear motion: ^b4ff95

- Position $\rightarrow$ **angular displacement**, $\theta\, [\pu{rad}]$
- Velocity $\rightarrow$ **angular velocity**, $\omega\, [\pu{rad/s}]$
- Acceleration $\rightarrow$ **angular acceleration**, $\alpha\, [\pu{rad/s^2}]$

### Angular Velocity

We can express the object's position in polar coordinates at any given moment $t$. We know that $r = R$ remains constant. The object is moving at constant speed, so it turns by $2\pi$ radians every $t = T$ seconds:

$$
\begin{align}
r(t) &= R \\\\
\theta(t) &= t \left( \frac{2\pi}{T} \right)
\end{align}
$$

Here, $\frac{2\pi}{T}$ is the angular velocity:

$$
\omega(t) = \frac{2\pi}{T}
$$

So the angular displacement is found similarly to linear displacement:

$$
\theta(t) = \omega t
$$

Notice that the time derivative of arc length must be the tangential speed, $v_{t}\left[\pu{m/s}\right]$. So we can relate tangential velocity and angular velocity by the radius

$$
\frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}R\theta
$$

$$
v_{t}=R\omega
$$

### Tangential Velocity

Then we can see that in linear coordinates,

$$
\begin{align}
x(t) &= r\cos\theta = R\cos(\omega t) \\\\
y(t) &= r\sin\theta = R\sin(\omega t)
\end{align}
$$

So we can find the position vector by combining the $x$ and $y$ components:

$$
\mathbf{r}(t) = R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\imath}} + R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}
$$

And the velocity by differentiating with respect to time:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{v}(t) &= \frac{d}{dt} \mathbf{r}(t) \\\\
&= \frac{d}{dt} \left[ R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\imath}} + R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}} \right] \\\\
\end{align}
$$

$$
\mathbf{v}(t)= -\omega R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\imath}} + \omega R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}
$$

^b88c36

Notice the direction of the velocity:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}} = \langle -\sin(\omega t), \cos(\omega t) \rangle
$$

indicates that it's tangential to the position. For this reason, we call it the **tangential velocity**.

The speed, which we denote $v$, is the magnitude of the velocity vector:

$$
\begin{align}
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert &= \sqrt{(\omega R \cos(\omega t))^2 + (\omega R \sin(\omega t))^2} \\\\
&= R\omega \sqrt{\cos^2(\omega t) + \sin^2(\omega t)} \\\\
v &= R\omega
\end{align}
$$

Or alternatively:

$$
\omega = \frac{v}{R}
$$

## Centripetal Acceleration

> [!def] Centripetal Acceleration
> Consider a mass $m\, [\pu{kg}]$ in uniform circular motion at radius $R\, [\pu{m}]$ with tangential velocity $v\, [\pu{m/s}]$ and angular velocity $\omega\, [\pu{rad/s}]$. The object experiences a radially inward **centripetal acceleration** with magnitude:
>
> $$
> a_c = \frac{v^2}{R} = \omega^2 R
> $$

So acceleration (the change in velocity) must be the time derivative of [[#^b88c36]].

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{a}(t) &= \frac{d}{dt} \left[ -\omega R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\imath}} + \omega R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}} \right] \\\\
&= -\omega^2 R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\imath}} - \omega^2 R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that the direction is:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{a}} = \langle -\cos(\omega t), -\sin(\omega t) \rangle
$$

which is the negative of:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}} = \langle \cos(\omega t), \sin(\omega t) \rangle
$$

This confirms that the acceleration must point directly towards the center of the turn. This is the **centripetal acceleration**.

The magnitude of the **centripetal acceleration** is:

$$
\begin{align}
\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert &= \sqrt{(-\omega^2 R \cos(\omega t))^2 + (-\omega^2 R \sin(\omega t))^2} \\\\
&= \omega^2 R \sqrt{\cos^2(\omega t) + \sin^2(\omega t)} \\\\
a_c &= \omega^2 R
\end{align}
$$

Or,

$$
\begin{align}
a_c &= \left( \frac{v}{R} \right)^2 R \\\\
a_c &= \frac{v^2}{R}
\end{align}
$$

Alternatively, using $v = R\omega$:

$$
a_c = \frac{v^2}{R}
$$

### Tangential Acceleration

An object can also experience tangential acceleration, which increases it's tangential velocity without affecting it's rotation. We can find a similar relation for angular acceleration and tangential acceleration $a_{t}\left[\pu{m/s^{2}}\right]$.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}v_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}R\omega
$$

$$
a_{t}=R\alpha
$$
