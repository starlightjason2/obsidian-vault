# 1.3 - Newton's Laws

**Dynamics** is the study of how forces affect the motion of objects.

> [!def] Force
> Force $\mathbf{F}\left[\pu{N}\right]$ is a [[2.1 - Vectors#^column-vector]] measured in *newtons* $[\pu{N}] = [\pu{kg \cdot m/s^2}]$. Force describes the push or pull on an object, either contact or long range.
>
> We can illustrate the forces on an object by drawing a force diagram, also called a free body diagram. We label each external force vector with an arrow. The net external force on an object is found by taking the vector sum of all outside forces acting on it:
> $$\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 + \dots$$

^force

## Newton's Laws

### Newton's First Law

> [!thm] Newton's First Law
> An object in motion stays in motion and an object at rest stays at rest unless acted upon by a net external [[#^force]].

^newtons-first-law

### Newton's Second Law

> [!thm] Newton's Second Law
> An [[4.0 - ODEs#^ode]] for the motion $\mathbf{x}(t)$ of an object. The net external [[#^force]] equals the mass $m$ times the acceleration $\ddot{\mathbf{x}}(t)$. Equivalently, the net external force equals the change in [[1.6 - Linear Momentum and Collisions#^momentum]] $\mathbf{p}$.
>
> $$
> \sum \mathbf{F} =m\,\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{x}}{dt^{2}}=\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}
> $$

^newtons-second-law

Only **external forces** can change an object's acceleration. In other words, you can't push a car from the inside and expect it to move!

Furthermore, only **net forces** can change an object's acceleration. So if all the net external forces cancel out, the object is in **equilibrium**.

> [!def] Equilibrium
> A mass $m \left[\pu{kg}\right]$ is in **equilibrium** in the absence of a net force, and obeys [[#^newtons-first-law]]:
>
> $$
> \sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}
> $$

### Newton's Third Law

Whenever one body exerts a force on another body, the first body experiences a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

> [!thm] Newton's Third Law
> Let $\mathbf{F}_{AB}\left[\pu{N}\right]$ be the [[#^force]] body $A$ exerts on body $B$, and let $\mathbf{F}_{BA}\left[\pu{N}\right]$ be the force body $B$ exerts on body $A$. Then:
>
> $$
> \mathbf{F}_{AB} = -\mathbf{F}_{BA}
> $$

This means the **Earth exerts the same force on the Moon** as the Moon does on the Earth. These forces do not cancel, since each body experiences only one of them.

## Non-conservative Forces

A force that's path-dependent is called a **non-conservative** force. Friction is a non-conservative force.

### Friction

If an object is being pulled by a force $\mathbf{F}_p$ and experiencing equal and opposite friction $\mathbf{F}_k$, then the object is in equilibrium:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_p + \mathbf{F}_k = 0
$$

#### Static Friction

To move an object, we must overcome maximum static friction: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{pull}} > \mathbf{F}_{\text{static}}$. The static force equals the force applied until this maximum threshold is reached and the object accelerates from equilibrium. The maximum force of static friction is proportional to the normal force; $\mu_s$ is **dimensionless** and must be determined experimentally.

> [!def] Static Friction
> Consider an object at rest on a surface. Let $\mu_s$ be the coefficient of static friction between the object and the surface, $\mathbf{F}_N\left[\pu{N}\right]$ be the normal force, and $\mathbf{F}_{\text{app}}\left[\pu{N}\right]$ the applied force. Then:
>
> $$
> \lVert \mathbf{F}_s \rVert \leq \mu_s \lVert \mathbf{F}_N \rVert
> $$

#### Kinetic Friction

An object in motion experiences **kinetic friction**. To keep it moving at constant velocity, $\mathbf{F}_{\text{pull}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{kinetic}}$ and the object remains in equilibrium. Again, $\mu_k$ is dimensionless and determined experimentally.

> [!def] Kinetic Friction
> Consider an object in motion on a surface. Let $\mu_k$ be the coefficient of kinetic friction and $\mathbf{F}_N\left[\pu{N}\right]$ the normal force. Then:
>
> $$
> \lVert \mathbf{F}_k \rVert = \mu_k \lVert \mathbf{F}_N \rVert
> $$

## Spring Force

When extended a certain displacement $\Delta y$ past **equilibrium**, the restoring spring force is proportional to the displacement. The **spring constant** $k$ is unique for every spring, and measures the "stretchability" of the spring.

> [!thm] Hooke's Law
> The spring force $\mathbf{F}$ is always in the opposite direction of $\Delta x$
> $$\mathbf{F}_{\text{spring}} = -k\Delta x$$

^hookes-law

## Universal Law of Gravitation

Forces that are **path-independent** are called **conservative** forces. Gravity is a **conservative**, long distance force.

> [!thm] Universal Law of Gravitation
> Let $m_1\left[\pu{kg}\right]$ and $m_2\left[\pu{kg}\right]$ be two masses separated by distance $r\left[\pu{m}\right]$. Then:
>
> $$
> \mathbf{F}_g = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}
> $$
>
> where $G \approx \pu{6.674 \times 10^{-11} N \cdot m^2 / kg^2}$.

## Solving Problems with Newton's Laws

### Atwood Machine

> [!exm] Atwood Machine
> An Atwood Machine is a simple two mass pulley system. Let $m_1$ and $m_2$ be two constant masses where $m_2$ is the heavier mass.
>
> The system moves as one, so both masses have the same magnitude of acceleration, which we can call $a$. However, the direction of acceleration of $m_2$ is down, and the direction of acceleration of $m_1$ is up. So then,
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> \mathbf{F}_{net_{m_{1}}}&=m_{1}a =\mathbf{F}_{T}-m_{1}g\\
> \mathbf{F}_{{net_{m2}}}&=-m_{2}a=\mathbf{F}_{T}-m_{2}g
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> So solving for tension, $\mathbf{F}_{T}=m_{1}a+m_{1}g$. We can now derive an expression for $a$:
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
 > -m_{2}a&=m_{1}a+m_{1}g-m_{2}g \\
> m_{1}a+m_{2}a &=m_{2} g-m_{1}g\\
> 
> a&=\frac{g(m_{2}-m_{1})}{(m_{1}+m_{2})}
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> If $m_{2}=M+\Delta m$ and $m_{1}=M-\Delta m$ then
>
> $$
> a=g\frac{(M+\Delta m)-(M-\Delta m)}{(M-\Delta m)+(M+\Delta m)}=g \frac{2\Delta m}{2M}=\frac{g}{M} \Delta m
> $$
>
>

> [!exm] Elevator Ride
> Consider someone standing on a scale in an elevator. If the elevator is still, then the weight of the person would read as >$mg$. If the elevator is in free fall, then there is **no way to distinguish from being in space**. If the elevator is >accelerating upwards with acceleration $a_{y}$ then the weight read on the scale will just be net force.
>
>$$
>\sum F=mg+ma_{y}=mg\left( 1+\frac{a_{y}}{g} \right)
>$$
>
> If going down, then the sign of acceleration is flipped.
>
>$$
>\sum F=mg-ma_{y}=mg\left( 1-\frac{a_{y}}{g} \right)
>$$
>
