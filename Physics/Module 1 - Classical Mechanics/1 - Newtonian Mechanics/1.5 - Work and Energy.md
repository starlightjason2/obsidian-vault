# 1.5 - Work and Energy

> [!def] Work
> Work $W(x)\left[\pu{J}\right]$ is the [[3.10 - Vector Integrals#^4bd53c]] of [[1.3 - Newton's Laws#^73f9cb]] $\mathbf{F}$ over a distance curve $C$, measured in *joules* $\left[\pu{J}\right]$.
>
> $$W_{AB}=\int_{C} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}$$
>
> If the force is a constant $\mathbf{F}$ over a displacement $\mathbf{d}$ with an angle $\theta$ between them, the work becomes
> $$W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}=\lVert{\mathbf{F}}\rVert\lVert{\mathbf{d}}\rVert\cos\theta$$

^e395c7

Notice that if the force and displacement are in the same direction, the work ($\theta=0$) is **positive**, like for an object in free fall (in that case, we say gravity is doing positive work). If they're opposed ($\theta=\pi$), the work is **negative**, like when an object is thrown into the air (we say gravity is doing negative work). If they're orthogonal ($\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}$), the work is **zero**.

## Energy

Energy is the **ability to do work**. Energy can be **transferred without loss** from one type to another within a system. Energy can also be **transferred into and out** of the system without loss.

### Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion for an object with mass $m\left[\pu{kg}\right]$. It's the work that must be done to accelerate the object from rest to speed $v\left[\pu{m/s}\right]$.

$\mathbf{F}$ is always in the same direction as $d\mathbf{x}$, so we can simplify the dot product to a multiplication, and use the chain rule for velocity to get acceleration in terms of $\mathbf{x}$.

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} &= \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{d\mathbf{x}}\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{v} \frac{dv}{d\mathbf{x}} \\
\mathbf{F} &= m\mathbf{a} = m\mathbf{v}\left( \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{d\mathbf{x}} \right)
\end{align}
$$

Now we plug this into the work equation:

$$
\begin{align}
\text{KE} &= \int_{x_i}^{x_f} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{x} \\
&= m\int_{x_i}^{x_f} \mathbf{v}\cdot\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dx},dx \\
&= m\int_{v_i}^{v_f} \mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{v} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}m v_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}m v_i^2
\end{align}
$$

If we assume that the object starts from rest, then $\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$. Furthermore, because we're squaring it, we only care about the magnitude of $\mathbf{v}$, or the speed $v$.

> [!def] Kinetic Energy
> Kinetic energy is the energy of motion for an object with mass $m\left[\pu{kg}\right]$ and speed $v\left[\pu{m/s}\right]$.
>
> $$
> \text{KE} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
> $$

^95e31f

> [!thm] Work-Energy Theorem
> The **net [[#^e395c7]]** done on an object equals its **change in kinetic energy** $\Delta\text{KE}$.
> $$\sum{W}=\Delta\text{KE}$$

^2bd330

### Potential Energy

A ball at the top of a hill can easily be perturbed and fall, gaining energy as it reaches the bottom. When moving it back up the hill, we must provide equivalent energy to work against gravity. Therefore we say that at the top, the object has **high potential energy**, and at the bottom, it has **low potential energy**.

Let's formalize this mathematically. Let $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}$ be the **bottom** of the hill, where we define potential energy $U(\mathbf{r})$ to be $U(\mathbf{a})=0$, and let $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}$ be the **top** of the hill. Let $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})$ be a vector field where each point in space $\mathbf{r}$ maps to a force $\mathbf{F}$, so an object at $\mathbf{r}$ will **accelerate in the direction of $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})$** if left alone.

To get the ball up the hill (from $\mathbf{a}$ to $\mathbf{b}$) at constant velocity, we must apply an opposite force $-F(\mathbf{r})$. The object's potential energy at $\mathbf{b}$ is equal to the work done by that force.

$$
U(\mathbf{b})=W_{ab}=\int_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathbf{b}}-F(\mathbf{r})\cdot d\mathbf{r}
$$

Or,

$$U(\mathbf{b})=-\int_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathbf{b}}F(\mathbf{r})\cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

> [!def] Potential Energy
> Potential energy $U\left[\pu{J}\right]$ is an object's **positional energy** within a **conservative force field** $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})$. Define a **reference point** $\mathbf{r}*{0}$ where $U(\mathbf{r}*{0})=0$. The potential energy is equal to the [[#^e395c7]] $W_{\mathbf{r}}$ done to move an object from $\mathbf{r}_{0}$ to $\mathbf{r}$.
>
> $$U(\mathbf{r})=-W(\mathbf{r})$$
>
> The object moves from high to low potential in the presence of the field.
>
> $$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})=-\nabla U(\mathbf{r})$$

^0663fa

### Gravitational

Assume that gravity is a **constant downward force** $\mathbf{F}*{g}=-F*{g},\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}$. Define the ground $y=0$ to have **zero potential**. At a point $\mathbf{p}=h,\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}$ above the ground,

$$
\begin{align}
U_{g}&=-\int_{0}^{\mathbf{h}}-F_{g},\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \\
&=\int_{0}^{h}F_{g}, dy \\
&= mgh
\end{align}
$$

> [!def] Gravitational Potential Energy
> Gravitational potential energy is the [[#^0663fa]] energy of a mass $m$ at a height $h$ above ground.
>
> $$
> U_{g}= mgh
> $$

^e2ef69

### Springs

Define the **equilibrium position** ($\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{0}$) to have zero potential. We can find the force with [[1.3 - Newton's Laws#^d81fd1]]. Then at a position $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{d}$, where the magnitude of the displacement is $\lVert{\mathbf{d}}\rVert=x$,

$$
\begin{align}
U_{\text{spring}} &=-\int_{0}^{\mathbf{d}}F_{\text{spring}}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \\
&=\int_{0}^{\mathbf{d}}k\mathbf{d}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \\
&=\frac{1}{2}kx^{2}
\end{align}
$$

> [!def] Spring Potential Energy
> **Spring potential energy** is the [[#^0663fa]] of a mass on a spring with spring constant $k$ at a displacement $x$ from equilibrium.
>
> $$
> \text{PE}_{\text{spring}} = \frac{1}{2}kx^{2}
> $$

^52a723

## Conservation of Mechanical Energy

**Mechanical energy** is the energy of movement and position. It's a combination of **kinetic** (energy of motion) and **gravitational potential** energy (energy stored in the position of an item relative to the ground).

> [!def] Mechanical Energy
> Mechanical energy $\text{ME}\left[\pu{J}\right]$ is a combination of [[#^95e31f]] and [[#^0663fa]].
>
> $$\text{ME} = \text{PE} + \text{KE}$$

^809d1b

> [!thm] Conservation of Mechanical Energy
> [[#^809d1b]] is **conserved** in systems without non-conservative forces.
>
> $$\text{KE}_i+\text{PE}_i=\text{KE}_f+\text{PE}_f$$

^bcffb2

So for a cart at the top of a rollercoaster, all the energy is in the form of potential energy — once it reaches the bottom, all of it is converted to kinetic energy.

## Non-Mechanical Energy

This is the energy of friction, deformation, heat, sound, etc. This energy is not conserved (in a simplified classical system) and is lost forever. Realistically, mechanical energy will be lost over time as non-mechanical energy.

### Thermal Energy

The thermal energy is the combined potential and kinetic energy of the atoms that comprise an object. The thermal energy depends on temperature, and we can define temperature as the **average kinetic energy** of the atoms in the system.

$$
W_{\text{external}} = \Delta E_{\text{system}} = \Delta \text{KE}*{\text{macro}} + \Delta E*{\text{thermal}}
$$

Forces like friction and drag cause the kinetic energy to be dissipated as thermal energy ($\Delta E_{\text{thermal}}$). So for example, if you're dragging something over a surface with friction, and that object moves at constant velocity, all the work you're doing turns into thermal energy.

## Power

> [!def] Power
> **Power** $P\left[\pu{W}\right]$ is [[#^e395c7]] $W$ over time.
> $$P=\frac{dW}{dt}$$
