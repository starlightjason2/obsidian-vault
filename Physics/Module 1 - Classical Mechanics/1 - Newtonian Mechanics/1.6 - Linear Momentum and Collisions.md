# 1.6 - Linear Momentum and Collisions

> [!def] Momentum
> An object with mass $m\left[\pu{kg}\right]$ and velocity $\mathbf{v}\left[\pu{m/s}\right]$ has momentum $p\left[\pu{kg\cdot m/s}\right]$.
>
> $$
> \mathbf{p} = m\Delta \mathbf{v}
> $$

^57c92e

Momentum ($p\left[\pu{kg\cdot m/s}\right]$) is a measurement of mass and velocity, and it falls out of Newton's Second Law.

$$
\begin{align}
F = ma_x &= m \frac{dv_x}{dt} \\
m,dv_x &= F_x,dt \\
m\int_{v_i}^{v_f} dv_x &= m\Delta v
\end{align}
$$

## Impulse

> [!def] Impulse
> **Impulse** $J[\pu{N\cdot s}]$ is the change in momentum and the integral of force $\mathbf{F}\left[\pu{N}\right]$ over time $t\left[\pu{s}\right]$:
>
> $$
> \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \mathbf{F} , dt = \Delta \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{J}
> $$

> [!thm] Newton's Second Law with Momentum
> If $\mathbf{F}$ is **force** and $\mathbf{p}$ is the [[#^57c92e]],
>
> $$
> \sum\mathbf{F} =\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}
> $$

^6257fb

Let's substitute momentum into [[1.3 - Newton's Laws#^aee106]]:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{F} &= m\mathbf{a} \\
&= m \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} \\
&= \frac{d}{dt}(m\mathbf{v})
\end{align}
$$

Or, in other words, a **force** creates a **change in momentum**.

$$
\mathbf{F} = \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}
$$

In a one-dimensional problem with a constant force $F(t)$, the **impulse** is just the change in momentum:

$$
F \Delta t = J = \Delta p
$$

So when a glass falls on concrete and breaks, its momentum must be changed by an impulse. The collision time $\Delta t$ is very small, so the force $F$ must be very large, causing the glass to break. Dropping it on a trampoline extends $\Delta t$, reducing $F$ and possibly preventing breakage. Similarly, a seatbelt will increase the $\Delta t$ of your deceleration, and the $\Delta F_{\text{avg}}$ will be smaller (and save your life).

We can also write **kinetic energy** in terms of momentum:

$$
\begin{align}
\text{KE} &= \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{(mv)^2}{2m} \\
&= \frac{p^2}{2m}
\end{align}
$$

## Collisions and Conservation of Momentum

Momentum is a **conserved** quantity in physics, so the **total momentum** of all objects in a system will **remain constant**. Collisions don't always conserve energy, but all collisions conserve momentum.

> [!thm] Conservation of Momentum
> The total [[#^57c92e]] of a system is conserved. If $\mathbf{p_i}$ and $\mathbf{p_f}$ are the initial and final total momentum of all particles in the system, then
> $$\mathbf{p_i} =\mathbf{p_f}$$

^bf7448

### Elastic Collisions

> [!def] Elastic Collision
> **Kinetic energy** is **conserved** in an **elastic collision**. Let $m_1$ and $m_2$ be two masses with initial velocities $v_{1,i},\ v_{2,i}$ and final velocities $v_{1,f},\ v_{2,f}$. By the [[#^bf7448]],
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> v_{1,f} &= \frac{v_{1,i}(m_2 - m_1) + 2m_2 v_{2,i}}{m_1 + m_2} \ \\
> v_{2,f} &= \frac{2m_1 v_{1,i} + v_{2,i}(m_2 - m_1)}{m_1 + m_2}
> \end{align}
> $$

Kinetic energy is conserved in an elastic collision. Consider the elastic collision of two masses, $m_1$ and $m_2$, with initial velocities $v_{1,i},\ v_{2,i}$ and final velocities $v_{1,f},\ v_{2,f}$.

We have one equation for conservation of energy:

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{2}m_1v_{1,i}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2v_{2,i}^2 &= \frac{1}{2}m_1v_{1,f}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2v_{2,f}^2 \\
m_1(v_{1,i}^2 - v_{1,f}^2) &= m_2(v_{2,f}^2 - v_{2,i}^2) \ \\
m_1(v_{1,i} - v_{1,f})(v_{1,i} + v_{1,f}) &= m_2(v_{2,f} - v_{2,i})(v_{2,f} + v_{2,i}) \tag{1}
\end{align}
$$

^6216e8

And another for conservation of momentum:

$$
\begin{align}
m_1v_{1,i} + m_2v_{2,i} &= m_1v_{1,f} + m_2v_{2,f} \ \\
m_1(v_{1,i} - v_{1,f}) &= m_2(v_{2,f} - v_{2,i}) \tag{2}
\end{align}
$$

^f75f4d

Dividing equation [[#^6216e8]] by [[#^f75f4d]] we get:

$$
v_{1,i} + v_{1,f} = v_{2,f} + v_{2,i} \tag{3}
$$

^e30691

So the sum of the velocities of the first object equals the sum of the velocities for the second object. This is a remarkable result on its own.

Multiply equation [[#^e30691]] by $m_1$ and add it to equation [[#^f75f4d]] to get an expression for $v_{2,f}$:

$$
\begin{align}
m_1v_{1,i} + m_2v_{2,i} &= m_2v_{2,f} \cancel{+ m_1v_{1,f}} \tag{2} \\
m_1v_{1,i} - m_1v_{2,i} &= m_1v_{2,f} \cancel{- m_1v_{1,f}} \tag{3} \\
\hline
2m_1v_{1,i} + m_2v_{2,i} - m_1v_{2,i} &= m_1v_{2,f} + m_2v_{2,f} \tag{2 + 3} \\
2m_1v_{1,i} + v_{2,i}(m_2 - m_1) &= v_{2,f}(m_1 + m_2) \ \\
\frac{2m_1v_{1,i} + v_{2,i}(m_2 - m_1)}{m_1 + m_2} &= v_{2,f}
\end{align}
$$

Multiply equation [[#^e30691]] by $-m_2$ and add it to equation [[#^f75f4d]] to get an expression for $v_{1,f}$:

$$
\begin{align}
m_1v_{1,i} + m_2v_{2,i} &= m_1v_{1,f} \cancel{+ m_2v_{2,f}} \tag{2} \\

* m_2v_{1,i} + m_2v_{2,i} &= m_2v_{1,f} \cancel{- m_2v_{2,f}} \tag{3} \\
  \hline
  m_1v_{1,i} - m_2v_{1,i} + 2m_2v_{2,i} &= m_2v_{1,f} + m_1v_{1,f} \tag{2 + 3} \\
  v_{1,i}(m_2 - m_1) + 2m_2v_{2,i} &= v_{1,f}(m_1 + m_2) \ \\
  \frac{v_{1,i}(m_2 - m_1) + 2m_2v_{2,i}}{m_1 + m_2} &= v_{1,f}
  \end{align}
  $$

We now have expressions for the final velocities of both objects in terms of the initial velocities and the two masses.

We get simpler expressions for collisions where one object is initially at rest. Assume $v_{2,i} = 0$ and let $v_{1,i} = v_i$.

$$
\begin{align}
v_{1,f} &= v_i \left( \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \right) \ \\
v_{2,f} &= v_i \left( \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2} \right)
\end{align}
$$

### Inelastic Collisions

> [!def] Inelastic Collision
> An **inelastic collision** is one where the colliding objects stick together (or deform), and **kinetic energy is not conserved**. Let $m_1\left[\pu{kg}\right]$ and $m_2\left[\pu{kg}\right]$ be two masses with initial velocities $v_{1,i}\left[\pu{m/s}\right],\ v_{2,i}\left[\pu{m/s}\right]$ and final velocities $v_{1,f}\left[\pu{m/s}\right],\ v_{2,f}\left[\pu{m/s}\right]$. By the [[#^bf7448]],$$
> v_f = \frac{m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i}}{m_1 + m_2}
> $$

^f29158

Kinetic energy is **not conserved** in an **inelastic collision**. Some of the kinetic energy is lost as heat, sound, or deformation. In an inelastic collision problem, it's common for the colliding objects to stick together.

Consider two masses, $m_1$ and $m_2$, with initial velocities $v_{1,i}$ and $v_{2,i}$, colliding and sticking together after the collision with a common final velocity $v_f$. ^f27396

We use conservation of momentum

$$
m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i} = (m_1 + m_2) v_f
$$

And solve for the final velocity.

$$
v_f = \frac{m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i}}{m_1 + m_2}
$$

Since the objects stick together, the system loses some kinetic energy during the collision. The initial and final kinetic energies are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{KE_i} &= \frac{1}{2} m_1 v*{1,i}^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_2 v_{2,i}^2 \\
\text{KE}_{f} &= \frac{1}{2} (m_1 + m_2) v_f^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the collision is inelastic,
$$\Delta \text{KE} = \text{KE_f} - \text{KE_i} \leq0$$

### Explosions

> [!def] Explosion
>  An explosion is a collision where the final energy $\text{KE_f}$ is **higher** than the initial energy $\text{KE_i}$. Say the explosion creates $n\in \mathbb{Z}^+$ particles and let $\mathbf{p}_{k}$ be the momentum of the $k$th particle.
>
> $$\sum_{k=0}^n\mathbf{p}_{k}=\mathbf{0}$$

So more energy comes out of the system than what the system starts with.  The energy comes from whatever caused the explosion, like the energy in the chemical bonds.
$$\Delta \text{KE} = \text{KE}*\text{initial} - \text{KE}*\text{final} \geq 0$$
