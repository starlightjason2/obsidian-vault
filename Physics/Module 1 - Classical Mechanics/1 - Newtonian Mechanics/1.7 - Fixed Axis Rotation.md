# 1.7 - Fixed Axis Rotation

> [!def] Center of Mass
> The center of mass is the average position of the total mass $M$ of the $n$ particles of an object, weighted by each particle's mass. Let $m_{k}$, $\mathbf{r}_{k}$ be the mass and position of the $k$th particle. Let $\mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} \left[\pu{m}\right]$ be the position of the center of mass.
>
> $$
> \mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=0}^{n} m_{k} \mathbf{r}_{k}
> $$
>
> For continuous objects, the center of mass is found with an integral:
>
> $$
> \mathbf{r}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M} \int \mathbf{r} \, dm
> $$

^70740f

We now discuss the rotational physics of rigid bodies—objects that do not deform. We can create analogs to linear motion, similar to what we did in [[1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion]].

- Mass → **moment of inertia**, $I \left[\pu{kg\cdot m^{2}}\right]$
- Force → **torque**, $\mathbf{\tau} \left[\pu{N\cdot m}\right]$

## Rotational Kinematics

> [!thm] Rotational Kinematic Equations
> Let $\Delta\theta$ be the [[1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion#^bc0269]], $\omega_{f}$, $\omega_{0}$ be the final and initial [[1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion#^475e51]]. Under constant [[1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion#^fb8801]] $\alpha$, we can reproduce the rotational equivalents of the kinematic equations.
>
> $$
> \Delta\theta = \omega_{0} t + \frac{1}{2} \alpha t^{2}
> $$
>
> ^db0aac
>
> $$
> \omega_{f} = \omega_{i} + \alpha t
> $$
>
> ^bfbdf1
>
> $$
> \omega_{f}^{2} = \omega_{i}^{2} + 2\alpha \Delta\theta
> $$
>
> ^6ae031
>
> $$
> \Delta\theta = \frac{\omega_{0} + \omega_{f}}{2} t
> $$
>

## Rotational Kinetic Energy

> [!def] Moment of Inertia
> The **moment of inertia** $I \left[\pu{kg\cdot m^2}\right]$ of an object is the rotational equivalent of mass. It's a measure of how hard it is to change rotational velocity. Let $R$ be the distance from the axis of rotation, and let the object be comprised of $n$ particles, with the $k$th particle having mass $m_{k}$.
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> I &= \sum_{k=0}^{n} m_{k} R^{2} =\iiint R^{2}dm
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> ^a7ee23

> [!def] Rotational Kinetic Energy
> Rotational kinetic energy $KE_{\text{rot}}$ is the energy associated with the rotational motion of a rigid body. Let $\omega$ be the [[1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion#^475e51]] and $I$ be the [[#^a7ee23]].
>
> $$
> KE_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^{2}
> $$

^262e33

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^262e33]]
Similar to translational kinetic energy, we can quantify the energy stored in rotational motion.

If the center of rotation is the origin, then a point at radius $r=R$ moves with speed $v_{t} = R\omega$. Substitute the moment of inertia.

$$
\begin{align}
KE_{\text{rot}} &= \frac{1}{2} mv_{t}^{2} = \frac{1}{2} m\left( \omega r \right)^{2} =\frac{1}{2}I\omega^{2}
\end{align}
$$

`\end{proof}`

> [!thm] Parallel Axis Theorem
> Let $m$ be the **mass** of an object and let $R$ be the **distance** from an **axis of rotation** through the **[[#^70740f]]** to a **new axis of rotation**. Then the [[#^a7ee23]] through the **new axis** is
> $$I_{\text{parallel-axis}}=I_{COM}+md^{2}$$

## Torque

> [!def] Torque
> Torque $\mathbf{\tau} \left[\pu{N\cdot m}\right]$ is the cross product of the force $\mathbf{F}$ and the position vector $\mathbf{r}$ from the **axis of rotation** to the **point where the force is applied**.
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> \mathbf{\tau} &= \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \\
> \lVert{\mathbf{\tau}}\rVert &=\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert \lVert{\mathbf{F}}\rVert \sin\theta
> \end{align}
> $$

^cbafb0

> [!thm] Newton's Second Law for Rotation
> Let $\ddot{\theta}$ be the [[1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion#^fb8801]], $I$ be the [[#^a7ee23]], $\mathbf{L}$ be the [[1.8 - Angular Momentum#^f050a1]], and $\vec{\tau}$ be the [[#^cbafb0]]. The rotational equivalent of [[1.3 - Newton's Laws#^aee106]] is:
>
> $$
> \sum \mathbf{\vec{\tau}} = I\,\frac{d^{2}\theta}{dt^{2}}=\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt}
> $$

^c9a8b3

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^c9a8b3]]
Torque is the rotational equivalent of force at distance $\mathbf{r}$ from the pivot. Substitute Newton's Second Law into the definition of torque.

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\tau}&=\mathbf{r}\times \mathbf{F} \\
&=\mathbf{r}\times (m\mathbf{a}) \\
&=\mathbf{r}\times m( \mathbf{a}r^{2})=mr^{2}(\mathbf{r}\times \mathbf{a}) \\
&=I\mathbf{\alpha}
\end{align}
$$

`\end{proof}`
