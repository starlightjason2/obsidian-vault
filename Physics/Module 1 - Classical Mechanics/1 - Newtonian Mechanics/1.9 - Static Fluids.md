# 1.9 - Static Fluids

**Fluids** yield to **shearing** and **sideways forces**. Both **liquids** and **gases** are considered fluids.

> [!def] Density
> The **average density** $\rho \left[\pu{kg/m^{3}}\right]$ is the mass per unit volume of a substance.
>
> $$\rho=\frac{m}{V}$$
>
> **Local density** is the density at a local region with infinitesimal volume. $$\rho=\lim_{\Delta V\to0}{\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta V}}$$

^36abd5

## Pressure

Fluids apply pressure whenever they're in contact with anything.

> [!def] Pressure
> **Pressure** $p\left[\pu{Pa}\right]$ or $p \left[\pu{N/m^{2}}\right]$ is the orthogonal force $F$ per unit area $A$ over which the force is applied.
>
> $$p=\frac{F}{A}$$
>
> At a specific point with infinitesimal area $dA$, the pressure is $$p=\frac{dF}{dA}$$

^732b3b

> [!def] Absolute Pressure
**Gauge pressure** $p_{g}$ is the pressure measured by a gauge, or a device that reads pressure.
>
**Atmospheric pressure** $p_{atm}$ is the pressure due to the atmosphere pressing down on us.
>
>  **Absolute pressure** $p_{abs}$ is the total pressure, which is the sum of the two.$$p_{abs}=p_{g}+p_{atm}$$

The idea of absolute pressure can be used to measure atmospheric pressure with a simple device (a barometer). Because the fluid is static, and assuming density is constant, we know that $p_{atm}=p_{abs}=h\rho g$.

### Variable Density

> [!thm] Pressure in a Fluid with Variable Density.
> A [[4.1.1 - First Order Linear ODEs#^655517]] for the [[#^732b3b]] of a fluid with [[#^36abd5]] $\rho(y)$, where $y$ is the height.
>
> $$\frac{dp}{dy}=-\rho(y)g$$
>
>

^f25916

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^f25916]]
The density of a fluid under a gravitational field will not be constant. The density will become higher as the gravitational force increases nearer a planet's core. We consider a **static** fluid, or a fluid that is under equilibrium. We know the mass $\Delta m$ is

$$
\Delta m=\rho (y)V=-\rho(y)\Delta y\Delta A
$$

^e31ba6

The minus sign is because $\Delta y$ is negative. Since the fluid is not moving, the forces between $y$ and $y+\Delta y$ are balanced.

$$
\begin{align}
p(y+\Delta y)\Delta A-p(y)\Delta A-g\Delta m&=0 \\
p(y+\Delta y)-p(y)&=g\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta A}
\end{align}
$$

Substitute [[#^e31ba6]],

$$
\begin{align}
p(y+\Delta y)-p(y)&=g\frac{-\rho(y)\Delta y\cancel{\Delta A}}{\cancel{\Delta A}} \\
\frac{p(y+\Delta y)-p(y)}{\Delta y}&=-\rho(y)g
\end{align}
$$

In the limiting case as $\Delta y \to 0$, this becomes a differential equation.
`\end{proof}`

## Pascal's Principle

> [!thm] Pascal's Principle
> Any **change in pressure** to an **enclosed fluid** is transmitted to **all points in the fluid**.

![[Pasted image 20250701164620.png]]

This does not mean that the pressure is the same everywhere. For example, consider the fluid enclosed in a piston. A change in pressure is applied by a weight at the top of the piston. By Pascal's Principle, the change in pressure at the top and bottom is the same, but the pressure at the bottom is higher due to the added pressure of the fluid.

![[Pasted image 20250701164601.png]]

This principle can be used to transmit pressure using fluids, and is the basis of **hydraulic systems**. A hydraulic jack will use a tube of liquid with one end's piston area smaller than the other $A_{1}<A_{2}\implies \frac{A_{1}}{A_{2}}<1$. Then, $F_{1}=F_{2} \frac{A_{1}}{A_{2}}\implies F_{1}<F_{2}$. If $F_{2}$ was the weight of a car, a smaller force $F_{1}$ could be applied to lift the car with a jack.

## Archimedes' Principle

A submerged object **displaces** a certain volume of fluid. If the object is removed, the displaced liquid reoccupies that lost space, and so the buoyant force experienced by an object must be the same as the weight of its displaced fluid.

> [!thm] Archimedes' Principle
> The buoyant force experienced by an object in a fluid is equal to the weight of the displaced fluid.

If the weight of an object is greater than the weight of it's displaced fluid, then the object will sink. In other words, an object will only float if its average (submerged) density is lower than the density of the fluid.
