# 4.1 - Oscillations

> [!def] Simple Harmonic Oscillator Equation
> The Simple Harmonic Oscillator Equation is a [[4.1.3 - Constant Coefficient ODEs#^ca6301]]. Equivalent to [[1.3 - Newton's Laws#^aee106]] for a mass experiencing a restoring force $F=\omega^{2}\,x$, where $\omega\left[\pu{Hz}\right]$ is the natural *resonant frequency*.
> $$\ddot{x}+\omega ^{2}\,x=0$$
>
> The solution is a [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^415797]]:
> $$x(t)=Ae^{i\omega t+\phi}$$

^6f684f

> [!def] Simple Harmonic Oscillator
> A simple harmonic oscillator is an object obeying the [[#^6f684f]] for any oscillatory variable $x$. 
> 
> The resulting motion is a [[3.7 - Periodic Functions#^4f8b8b]] for phase shift $\phi \left[\pu{rad}\right]$, amplitude $x=A$, and period $T=2\pi/\omega$.
> $$\begin{align}
x(t)&=A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \\
&=B_{1}\cos \omega t+B_{2} \sin \omega t
\end{align}$$
>
> The solution to a simple harmonic oscillator can also be written in the following forms for the [[3.6 - Complex Numbers#^29ae56|Complex Conjugates]] $C=\alpha\pm\beta i$, where $B_{1}=2\alpha$, $B_{2}=2\beta$, or as a single $D=Ae^{i\phi}$.
> $$\begin{align}
x(t)&=Ce^{i\omega t}+\bar{C}e^{-i\omega t} \\
&=\mathrm{Re}\Big[De^{i\omega t}\Big]
\end{align}$$
>
>

^d9cde8

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^d9cde8]]
See [[4.1.3 - Constant Coefficient ODEs#^382f29]]. Given $\ddot{x}(t)+(\omega_{0}) ^{2}\,x(t)=0$, the characteristic equation $r^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}=0$ has solutions found with the quadratic formula $r_{1,2}=\pm \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-4\omega_{0}^{2}}=\pm i\,\omega_{0}$. Using the even and odd properties of $\sin$ and $\cos$,

$$
\begin{align}
x(t)=c_{1}\,e^{i\omega t}+c_{2}\,e^{-i\omega t}&=c_{1}\cos( \omega t)+c_{2}\cos (-\omega t)+i(c_{1}\sin( \omega t)+c_{2}\sin(-\omega t)) \\
&=(c_{1}+c_{2})\cos \omega t+i(c_{1}-c_{2})\sin \omega t
\end{align}
$$

In order for $x(t)$ to be real, the [[3.6 - Complex Numbers#^29ae56]] of $c_{1}=C=\alpha+\beta i$ must be $c_{2}=\bar{C}=\alpha-\beta i$ so that $c_{1}+c_{2}=2\alpha\in \mathbb{R}$ and $i(c_{1}-c_{2})=-2i^{2}\beta=2\beta$.

$$
x(t)=2\alpha \cos \omega t+2\beta \sin \omega t
$$

Then we can also write $C=D\,e^{i\phi}, \ \bar{C}=D\,e^{-i\phi}$, so $x(t)=D\,(e^{i\phi }e^{\omega t}+\,e^{-i\phi }e^{-\omega t})$ and

$$
x(t)=D(e^{i(\omega t+\phi)}+e^{-i(\omega t+\phi)})
$$

And with the identity $\cos \theta=\frac{1}{2}(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})$, for $D=\frac{A}{2}$,

$$
x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)
$$

`\end{proof}`

## Energy

> [!thm] SHO Max Energy
> The maximum potential energy $U$ of a [[#^d9cde8]] with amplitude $A$ and force constant $k$ is the [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^e395c7]] done on it.
> $$U_{max}=\frac{1}{2}kA^{2}$$

^fe0773

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^fe0773]]
By the [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^2bd330]], the work done on a mass $m$ in SHO from $x=0$ to $x=A$ is the potential energy.

$$
\begin{align} \\
W&=\int \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =-k\int_0^{A}x\,dx=\frac{1}{2}kA^{2}
\end{align}
$$
`\end{proof}`

## Examples

> [!exm] Mass on a Horizontal Spring
> According to [[1.3 - Newton's Laws#^d81fd1]] for a spring with constant $k$,
>
> $$m\ddot{x}+kx=0$$
>
> Therefore, the mass is a [[#^d9cde8]] where $\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$, $T=2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$.
>

> [!exm] Classical Pendulum
> The only force is gravity. Use a first order [[6.2 - Taylor Series#^2e7735]] around $\theta=0$ (small angle approximation, $\theta<\pu{20deg}$).
>
> $$L\,\ddot{\theta}+g\sin\theta\approxeq\ddot{\theta}+\frac{g}{L}\,\theta = 0$$
>
> Therefore, the pendulum is a [[#^d9cde8]] with $\omega=\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}$, $T= 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$.

> [!example] Uniform Circular Motion
> Consider the **projection** of the position of an object in Uniform Circular Motion with radius $r=A$. Substitute [[1.2 - Uniform Circular Motion#^475e51]].
> $$x(t,\theta)=A\cos(\theta)$$
> Therefore, the $x$ component in uniform circular motion behaves like a [[#^d9cde8]].
