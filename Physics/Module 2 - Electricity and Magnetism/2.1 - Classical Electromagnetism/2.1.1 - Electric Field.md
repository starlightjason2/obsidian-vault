# 2.1.1 - Electric Field

## Charge and Force

**Electric charge** is the source of the **electric force**, also called the **Coulomb force**.

Like charges repel, and opposite charges attract. Charge is measured in Coulombs $\left[\pu{C}\right]$, and behaves like the "mass equivalent" in electromagnetism. A Coulomb is a very large amount of charge, with a lightning bolt measuring at about $\pu{10C}$.

> [!def] Electric Force
> Let $q_{1}, \ q_{2}$ be two *rest charges* $\left[\pu{C}\right]$, with $q_{1}$ at the origin and $q_{2}$ at $\mathbf{r}$. Let $k_{e}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}=\pu{8.987e9\frac{N.m^{2}}{C^{2}}}$. The [[1.3 - Newton's Laws#^force]] between $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ is
>
> $$
> \mathbf{F}=k_{e}\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert\,^{2}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
> $$

^electric-force

### Charge Distributions

> [!def] Charge Density
> A [[3.1 - Scalar Functions#^function]] representing the local **electric charge density** in a [[3.8 - Regions#^3d-bounded-region]].
>$$\begin{align}
\rho(\mathbf{r}), \ && \mathbf{r}\in \mathbb{R}^{3} \\
\sigma(\mathbf{r}), \ && \mathbf{r}\in \mathbb{R}^{2} \\
\lambda(\mathbf{r}), \ && \mathbf{r}\in \mathbb{R}^{1} \\
\end{align}$$

^b43a55

A continuous charge distribution is when we consider a mass of quantized charges in some distribution. Instead of charge, we can then consider the volume $V$ and the **charge density** $\rho$. The total charge $Q$ will be

$$
Q=\int{V}\rho \, dV
$$

We can utilize symmetry across spheres, cylinders, planes, etc. to simplify multiple integrals in space. Line density is commonly represented with $\lambda \left[\pu{C/m}\right]$, surface density with $\sigma \left[\pu{C/m^{2}}\right]$, and volume density with $\rho \left[\pu{C/m^{3}}\right]$. 

## Electric Fields

The **net electric force** on a charge will be the vector sum of all the electric forces on it. To make the force on a charge easy to calculate, we describe the effect of the total charges in space using a vector field.

> [!def] Electric Field
> Consider a charge $q$ at the origin. The electric field $\mathbf{E}\left[\pu{N/C}\right]$ is an inverse square vector field describing the strength of the [[#^electric-force]] per unit charge at a distance $\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert$.
>
> Electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and [[2.1.2 - Electric Potential#^electric-potential]] $V$ are related via the [[3.11 - Fundamental Theorems#^fundamental-theorem-of-line-integrals]], where $\mathbf{E}=-\nabla V$.
>
> $$\mathbf{E}=k_{e}\frac{q}{\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert\,^{2}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}=-\nabla V$$
>
>

^electric-field

> [!thm] Curl of the Electric Field
> The [[#^electric-field]] $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ obeys the [[3.3 - Vector Fields#^curl-test-for-conservative-fields]].
> $$\nabla \times \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})=0$$

For the purposes of calculations, it doesn't matter much where an electric field comes from. A field produced by multiple charges will have the same effect as a field produced by a single charge, if all the field vectors are the same.

### Electric Flux

The amount of electric field **passing orthogonal through a surface** is the **electric flux**. For any given normal vector $\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$, that amount is just $\mathbf{E}\cdot \,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$. The total electric flux is found by integrating across the surface.

> [!def] Electric Flux
> **Electric flux** $\Phi(\mathbf{r}) \left[\pu{N\cdot m^{2}/C}\right]$ is the [[3.10 - Vector Integrals#^vector-flux]] of the [[2.1.1 - Electric Field#^electric-field]] $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ through surface $S$.
>
> $$\Phi(\mathbf{r})=\iint_{S}{\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A}}$$

^electric-flux

## Gauss's Law

Charge is the source of the electric field, so it follows that it must be the **divergence** of $\mathbf{E}$ is the **charge density** (since the actual charge goes to zero in a limit). By the Divergence Theorem, it stands that the **electric flux** must be proportional to the **enclosed charge**.

> [!thm] Gauss's Law
> The [[#^electric-flux]] through $S$ is proportional to the enclosed charge $Q_{enc}$.
>
> $$\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_{0}},&& \iint_{S}{\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A}}=\frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{0}}
\end{align}$$
> By the [[3.11 - Fundamental Theorems#^divergence-theorem]], the divergence $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ over a **closed surface** is proportional to the [[#^b43a55]] $\rho(\mathbf{r})$.
> $$\nabla\cdot \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r})}{\epsilon_{0}}$$

^e32ff5

> [!cor] Gauss's Law with Symmetrical Charge Distribution
> For a **symmetrical charge distribution** with volume $V$ and surface area $A$, let the charge density be $\rho$ and the total enclosed charge be $Q=\rho V$. Let **the normal vectors $\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ be parallel to the [[2.1.1 - Electric Field#^electric-field]]**, creating an [[#^electric-flux]] $\phi$.
>
> $$
> \mathbf{E}=\frac{Q}{\phi\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{V\rho}{A\,\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
> $$

^gausss-law-with-symmetrical-charge-distribution

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^gausss-law-with-symmetrical-charge-distribution]]
We can use Gauss's Law to calculate the electric fields for **continuous charge distributions**. If we construct a surface **orthogonal** to the charge, then the normal vectors $\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ will be parallel to the electric field $\mathbf{E}$.  We can then simplify the integral because $\mathbf{E}\cdot \,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\lVert{E}\rVert$.

Let $A$ be the **surface area of $S$**, $V$ be the **volume enclosed by $S$**, and $\rho$ be the **charge distribution**. We can see that the total enclosed charge will be $Q_{enc}=V\rho$

$$
\begin{align}
\oiint_{S}{\mathbf{E}\cdot \,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\,da}&=\frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_{0}} \\
\oiint_{S}{\lVert{\mathbf{E}}\rVert \,da}&=\frac{V\rho}{\epsilon_{0}} \\
A\lVert{\mathbf{E}}\rVert&=\frac{V\rho}{\epsilon_{0}} \\
\end{align}
$$

$$
\mathbf{E}=\frac{V\rho}{\epsilon_{0}A}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{Q}{\epsilon_{0}A}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
$$

`\end{proof}`

### Spherical Symmetry

> [!exm] Gauss's Law with Spherical Symmetry
> Consider a continuous spherical charge distribution of radius $R$, charge density $\rho$, and total charge $Q$. Use a spherical Gaussian surface at distance $r$.
>
> The volume of the sphere is $V=\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}$, and the surface area of the Gaussian surface is $A=4\pi r^{2}$.
>
> Using the [[#^gausss-law-with-symmetrical-charge-distribution]], we find the electric field **inside the charged sphere** ($r<R$) is
>
> $$
> \mathbf{E}=\frac{\frac{\cancel{4}}{3}\cancel{\pi} r^{3}\rho}{\cancel{4\pi} r^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{r\rho}{3\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
> $$
>
> Meanwhile, the electric field **outside the charged sphere** ($r>R$) is equivalent to that of a **point charge**.
>
>
> $$\mathbf{E}=\frac{Q}{4\pi r^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{Q}{r^{2}}$$

### Rectangular Symmetry

> [!exm] Gauss's Law with Rectangular Symmetry
> Consider an infinite continuous planar charge distribution with charge density $\sigma$. Use a cuboidal Gaussian surface at a distance $r$ from the plane. The charge enclosed within will be $Q=\sigma r^{2}$.
>
> Realize the **flux through the sides will cancel out**, so we only consider the flux through the top and bottom, which will be $\phi=A+A=2r^{2}$.
>
> Using the [[#^gausss-law-with-symmetrical-charge-distribution]], we find the electric field is
>
> $$
> \mathbf{E}=\frac{\sigma r^{2}}{2r^{2}\,\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
> $$

^gausss-law-with-rectangular-symmetry

### Cylindrical Symmetry

> [!exm] Gauss's Law with Cylindrical Symmetry
> Consider an infinite continuous linear charge distribution with charge density $\lambda$. Use a cylinderical Gaussian surface with a radius $r$ and length $l$. The charge enclosed will be $Q=\lambda l$
>
> Realize the **flux through flat ends of the cylinder will cancel out**, so we only consider the flux through the side, which has surface area $A=2\pi rl$
>
> Using the [[#^gausss-law-with-symmetrical-charge-distribution]], we find the electric field is
>
> $$
> \mathbf{E}=\frac{\lambda\cancel{l}}{2\pi r\cancel{l}\,\epsilon_{0}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\,\epsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{r}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
> $$
