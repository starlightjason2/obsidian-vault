# 2.1.2 - Electric Potential

## Electric Potential

We want to keep creating abstractions so we no longer have to worry about point charges and can evaluate charge in aggregate. Electric potential is an abstraction of potential energy per charge, similar to how the electric field is an abstraction of electric force per charge. Potential represents the **level curves** of the **electric field**.

> [!def] Electric Potential
> The **electric potential** $V\left[\pu{J/C}\right]$ of a charge $q$ is is the [[#^electric-potential-energy]] per charge at any point $\mathbf{r}$.
>
> [[2.1.1 - Electric Field#^electric-field]] $\mathbf{E}$ and electric potential $V$ are related via the [[3.11 - Fundamental Theorems#^fundamental-theorem-of-line-integrals]], where $\mathbf{E}=-\nabla V$. Let $C$ be a curve from the origin to $\mathbf{r}$.
>
> $$
> V(\mathbf{r})=\int_C \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\frac{k_{e}\,q}{\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert\,}
> $$
>

^electric-potential

Since potential is a **scalar field**, it follows the principle of superposition, so the potential for a system of charges is the sum of the potential of each.

We can now consider the potential of a singular charge $q$, rather than a pair of charges like previously. We simply substitute the [[#^potential-energy-of-a-two-charge-system]] into the [[#^electric-potential]].

> [!cor] Electric Potential of a Point Charge
> Consider a point charge $q$ at point $\mathbf{r}'$. The [[#^electric-potential]] at $\mathbf{r}$ , assuming a **reference zero point** at **infinity**, is
>
> $$
> V(\mathbf{r})=k_{e}\sum_{k=1}^{n} {\frac{q_{k}}{\lVert{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}\rVert }}
> $$

^electric-potential-of-a-point-charge

> [!thm] Electric Potential of a Charge Distribution
> The [[#^electric-potential]] $V(\mathbf{r})$ is the [[2.1.1 - Electric Field#^b43a55]] $\rho(\mathbf{r'})$ over the separation $\lvert{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}\rvert\,$, integrated over the charge distribution.
>
> $$\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\iiint \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{\lvert{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}\rvert\,}dV'$$
>
> Note that $\mathbf{r}$ is a point the potential is measured and $\mathbf{r}'$ is a point where the charge density is measured.



## Electric Potential Energy

> [!def] Electric Potential Energy
> Electric [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^potential-energy]] $U$ is the energy of a charge $q$ in an [[2.1.1 - Electric Field#^electric-field]] $\mathbf{E}$. Let $\mathbf{r}_{0}$ be the **reference point** where $U(\mathbf{r}_{0})=0$. The potential energy of $q$ at point $p$ is
>
> $$
> U(\mathbf{r})=q\,V=-\int_{\mathbf{r}_{0}}^{\mathbf{r}} q\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{r}
> $$

^electric-potential-energy

### Two Charge System

> [!thm] Potential Energy of a Two Charge System
> Given a charge $Q$ at the origin and a charge $q$ at $\mathbf{r}\left[\pu{m}\right]$, the [[#^electric-potential-energy]] $U(\mathbf{r})$ of $q$ is
>
> $$
> U(\mathbf{r})=\frac{k_{e}Qq}{\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert }
> $$

^potential-energy-of-a-two-charge-system

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^potential-energy-of-a-two-charge-system]]

Consider a static point charge $Q$ and a dynamic point charge $q$. Place $Q$ at the origin. Let $q$ be at any point $\mathbf{r}$. As $q$ moves toward the origin, the electric force approaches infinity, and as $q$ moves away from the origin, the force approaches $0$. So we can define $\lim_{\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert\to\infty}{U(\mathbf{r})}=0$.

It's common, therefore, to **set the reference point at infinity** when calculating electric potential energy.

Consider a static point charge $Q$ and a dynamic point charge $q$. We want to find the [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^potential-energy]] of $q$ in relation to its displacement from $Q$.

$$
U(\mathbf{p})=-W_{AB}=-\int_{A}^{B}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\cdot d\mathbf{r}
$$

Set $A$ to $\infty$, where we define the potential as zero. Set $B$ to a point $\mathbf{p}$. Substitute [[2.1.1 - Electric Field#^electric-force]] for $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})$.

$$
\begin{align}
U(\mathbf{p}&=-\int_{\infty}^{\mathbf{p}}{\frac{k_{e}Qq}{\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert^{2}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot d\mathbf{r}}\\
&=-k_{e}Qq\int_{\infty}^{\mathbf{p}}{\frac{1}{\lVert{\mathbf{r}}\rVert ^{2}}\,d\mathbf{r}}\\
&=\left.\frac{k_{e}Qq}{\lVert{r}\rVert }\right\vert_{\infty}^{\mathbf{p}}\\
&=k_{e}Qq\left(\frac{1}{\lVert{\mathbf{p}}\rVert }- \lim_{r\to\infty}{\frac{1}{r}}\right)\\
&=\frac{k_{e}Qq}{\lVert{\mathbf{p}}\rVert}
\end{align}
$$

The solution is symmetric between $q$ and $Q$, so we have a final expression for the potential energy of a two charge system.
`\end{proof}`

## Electric Power

> [!thm] Electric Power
> Electric [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^power]] $P$ is the change in [[2.1.2 - Electric Potential#^electric-potential-energy]]. For a wire with a [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^voltage]] $V$, [[2.1.4 - Current#^current]] $I$, and [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^resistance]] $R$, the power gained is
>
> $$
> P=IV
> $$
>
> And the power lost by the resistors is
>
> $$
> P=\frac{V^{2}}{R}=I^{2}R
> $$

^electric-power

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^electric-power]]
Let's find the power needed to drive a current $I$ with a voltage $\Delta V$. Let the potential at the end of the circuit be the zero reference point, so the potential energy of a charge $q$ is $U=q\Delta V$.

$$
\begin{align}
P&=\frac{dU}{dt} =\frac{d}{dt}q\Delta V \\
&=\Delta V{\frac{dq}{dt}}=I\,\Delta V
\end{align}
$$

This is the power gained by the electrical components. By plugging in Ohm's Law, we also get the power lost by the resistors.
`\end{proof}`
