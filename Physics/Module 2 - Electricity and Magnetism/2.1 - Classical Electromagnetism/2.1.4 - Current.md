# 2.1.4 - Current

Applying a **voltage** to a wire will produce a **current** and some **resistance**. Resistance is like the electrical equivalent of friction. Materials have a property called **resistivity**, describing how much resistance they have.

> [!def] Current Density
> The current density $\mathbf{J}\left[\pu{C\,m/s}\right]$ is charge velocity $\mathbf{v}$ times the charge density $\rho$.
> $$\mathbf{J}=\rho \,\mathbf{v}$$

^current-density

> [!def] Current
> A **current** $I\left[\pu{C/s}\right]$ is the [[3.10 - Vector Integrals#^vector-flux]] of the [[2.1.4 - Current#^current-density]] through any flat surface (or "gate") $S$. Equivalently, current is the time [[3.5 - Derivatives#^derivative]] of charge.
>
> $$
> I=\iint_{S}\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{A}
> $$

^current

## Drift Velocity

> [!def] Drift Velocity
> The **drift velocity** $v_{d}$ of a charge $q$ is its **average velocity** in a [[2.1.4 - Current#^current|Current]] $I$. If there's $n$ charges per unit volume passing through a cross-sectional area $A$, then
>
> $$
> v_{d}=\frac{I}{nqA}
> $$

^drift-velocity

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^drift-velocity]]
Say that $v_{d}$ is the average velocity of a charge. If a wire with cross-sectional area $A$ has $n$ point charges per volume $V$, then each charge $q$ will move an average distance of $dx=v_{d}\,dt$.

The volume $V$ traversed in time $dt$ is $V=Av_{d}\,dt$. The total charge within that volume will be 

$$
Q=Vnq=Av_{d}nq\,dt
$$
$$
v_{d}=\frac{e\,\tau}{m_{e}}(V_{2}-V_{1})
$$
So the current is then,

$$
I=\frac{dQ}{dt}=A\,v_{d}\,n\,q =\frac{A\,e^{2}\,\tau \,n\,\Delta V}{m_{e}}
$$

Or, $I\propto \Delta V$

`\end{proof}`
