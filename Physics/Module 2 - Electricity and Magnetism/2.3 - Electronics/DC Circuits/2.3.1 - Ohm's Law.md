# 2.3.1 - Ohm's Law

A circuit is powered by a **voltage**, or difference in electric potential. The difference in potential drives electrons, similar to water flowing down a hill.

> [!def] Voltage
> **Voltage** $\Delta V\left[\pu{V}\right]$ is the difference in [[2.1.2 - Electric Potential#^4995c8]] between two points $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.
>
> $$\Delta V=V(\mathbf{b})-V(\mathbf{a})$$

^23952c

> [!def] Resistance
> **Resistance** $R\left[\pu{\ohm}\right]$ is the opposition experienced by charges as they flow through a conductor. For a wire with **resistivity** $\rho$, length $L$, and cross-sectional area $A$,
>
> $$
> R=\rho\frac{L}{A}
> $$

^995af2

> [!thm] Ohm's Law
> If a [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^23952c]] is the product of [[#^995af2]] $R$ and [[2.1.4 - Current#^45defa]] $I$. Materials that do not exhibit this relationship are called **non-ohmic**, and ones that do are called **ohmic**.
>
> $$
> V=IR
> $$

## Kirchhoff's Laws

> [!thm] Kirchhoff's Laws
> 1. Net [[2.1.4 - Current#^45defa]] into a junction equals net current out of the junction.
> 
> $$
> \sum_{\text{in}} I=\sum_{\text{out}}I
> $$
>
> 2. The [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^23952c]] drop across each component in *all possible* circuit loops $\{L \}$ is zero.
> 
> $$
> \forall n\in \{L \}, \ \sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}=0
> $$

## Resistors

### Series

All circuits have some resistance. We can model the simplest circuit as having a resistor $R$, a current $I$, and a voltage $V$, related by Ohm's Law. Resistors can be in **series** or in **parallel**.

> [!thm] Resistors in Series
> Two resistors in **series** with [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^995af2]] $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ have an **equivalent resistance** $R$.
>
> $$
> R=R_{1}+R_{2}
> $$

^b66ce3

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^b66ce3]]
The potential, starting at $V$, will drop across each resistor until it reaches $V=0$. So for two resistors in series, let the drop in resistance be $V_{1}$ and $V_{2}$.

$$
\begin{align}
V&=V_{1}+V_{2} \\
IR&=IR_{1}+IR_{2} \\
\end{align}
$$

$$
R=R_{1}+R_{2}
$$
`\end{proof}`

### Parallel

> [!thm] Resistors in Parallel
> Two resistors in **parallel** with [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^995af2]] $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ have an **equivalent resistance** $R$.
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{R}=\frac{1}{R_{1}}+\frac{1}{R_{2}}
> $$

^f5689f

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^f5689f]]
A circuit with two parallel resistors has **two loops**, so the current $I_{1},I_{2}$ splits at that junction before recombining into $I=I_{1}+I_{2}$. The potential is $V$ at the start of each loop, and drops to $0$ at the end. This means that potential across each resistor is $V_{1}=V_{2}=V=IR$.

$$
\begin{align}
I&=I_{1}+I_{2} \\
\frac{V}{R}&=\frac{V_{1}}{R_{1}}+\frac{V_{2}}{R_{2}} \\
\frac{\cancel{V}}{R}&=\frac{\cancel{V}}{R_{1}}+\frac{\cancel{V}}{R_{2}}
\end{align}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{R}=\frac{1}{R_{1}}+\frac{1}{R_{2}}
$$

An intuitive way to imagine this is a river flowing down a hill. Steams flowing down in parallel will have the same velocity at the end, but streams in series will flow faster, since there's a longer slope to fall down. Likewise, a river splits when it encounters a function, with the current splitting into separate flows.

### Voltage Divider

> [!thm] Voltage Divider
> A [[#^23952c]] divider has two [[#^b66ce3]] $R_{2}, \ R_{1}$, where source $V_{\mathrm{in}}=I(R_{1}+R_{2})$. The output $V_{\mathrm{out}}=V_{2}$ is the voltage drop across the second (load) resistor $V_{\mathrm{out}}=IR_{2}$.
> $$\frac{V_{\mathrm{out}}}{V_{\mathrm{in}}}  =\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}+R_{2}}$$

## Equivalent Circuits

> [!def] Open Circuit Voltage
> In a DC circuit, the [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^23952c]] when no [[2.1.4 - Current#^45defa]] is flowing is the **open circuit voltage** $V_{OC}$.

> In a DC circuit, the [[2.1.4 - Current#^45defa]] when no [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^23952c]]s applied is the **short circuit current** $I_{SC}$.
