# 2.3.3 - Capacitors

A capacitor is a device that stores charge. We measure how much charge it can store with **capacitance**.

> [!def] Capacitance
> Capacitance $C\left[\pu{F}\right],\pu{1F}=\frac{\pu{1C}}{1V}$ is charge $Q$ stored per [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^23952c]] $V$.
>
> $$
> C=\frac{Q}{V}
> $$

^3b3908

> [!thm] Capacitance of a Parallel Plate capacitor
> The [[#^3b3908]] of a parallel plate capacitor with plates separated by distance $d$ is
>
> $$
> C=\epsilon_{0}\frac{A}{d}
> $$

^f07486

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^f07486]]
A parallel plate capacitor is made of two conductive plates in proximity. We can approximate the plate as infinite when very close to it. Using [[2.1.1 - Electric Field#^5d9ed6]], the electric field for one plate is $\mathbf{E}=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_{0}}$, so the field between two plates is

$$
E=\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_{0}}
$$

We need the potential $V(d)$, where $d$ is the distance from the negative plate. Let $V(0)=0$.

$$
\begin{align}
V&=-\int_{0}^{d} \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =-\int_{0}^{d}{\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_{0}}}\,dr =-\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_{0}}\mathbf{d}
\end{align}
$$

The potential difference (or voltage) is $V(0)-V(d)\Delta V=\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_{0}}d$, which means the capacitance is

$$
\begin{align}
C&=\frac{Q}{V}=\frac{\sigma A}{V} =\cancel{\sigma} A\left( \frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\cancel{\sigma }d} \right) =\epsilon_{0}\frac{A}{d}
\end{align}
$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!def] Dielectric Constant
> A **dielectric** with constant $\kappa$ inserted between the places of a capacitor will decrease the voltage $V$ across it to $V_{d}$, with a new [[#^3b3908]] $C_{d}=C\kappa$.
>
> $$\kappa\equiv \frac{V}{V_{d}}$$
>
>

## Charge and Current

> [!def] Capacitor Time Constant
> The time constant for a capacitor given [[#^3b3908]] $C$ and [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^995af2]] $R$ is
> $$\tau\equiv CR$$

^095426

> [!thm] Capacitor Charge
> Consider an RC circuit with [[#^3b3908]] $C$ and [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^995af2]] $R$ in series. Let $RC=\tau$ be the [[#^095426]], $VC=Q_{0}$ be the initial charge of the capacitor , and $I_{0}=\frac{V}{R}$ initial current.
>
> While **charging**, the charge $q(t)$ of the capacitor and current $I(t)$ of the circuit is
> $$q(t)=Q_{0}(1-e^{-t/\tau})$$
> $$I=\frac{V}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
> And while **discharging**, its
> $$q(t)=Q_{0}(1-e^{-t/\tau})$$
> $$I(t)=-I_{0}e^{-t/\tau}$$

^4f56ad


`\begin{proof}`@[[#^4f56ad]]
We know the voltage across the capacitor, by definition, is $V_{C}=\frac{q}{C}$, and To complete the equation, we use [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^8cd332]].,

Then by [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^03a977]], the equation of motion is,

$$
V_{R}+V_{C}=IR+\frac{q}{c}
$$

 While charging, the capacitor will drop potential, behaving like a resistor. While discharging, it will add to the potential, acting like a battery.

$$
\begin{align} \\
IR+\frac{q(t)}{C}&=V(t) \\
\frac{dq}{dt}R+\frac{q(t)}{C}&=\frac{V(t)}{R} \\
\end{align}
$$

Use the [[4.1.1 - First Order Linear ODEs#^ff34cc]] where $\int p(t)\,dt=\int\frac{1}{RC}\,dt=\frac{t}{RC}$

$$
\begin{align}
e^{t/RC}\left( \frac{dq}{dt}+\frac{q(t)}{RC} \right)=e^{t/RC}\frac{V}{R} &\implies \frac{d}{dt}\left( e^{t/RC}\, q(t) \right)=e^{t/RC} \frac{V}{R} \\
e^{t/RC}\, q(t)&=\frac{V}{R}\int_{0}^{t} e^{t'/RC}\,dt' =\frac{V}{\cancel{R}}\,\cancel{R}C\,e^{t'/RC}\Big|_{0}^{t}  \\
q(t)&=VC\,(1-e^{-t/RC}) \\
\end{align}
$$

The charge is $0$ at the start at $t=0$, so $C_{2}=VC$. Let $\tau=RC$. This is the [[#^095426]] associated with charging and discharging.

Take the time derivative of both sides to find the current. Let $I_{0}=\frac{V}{R}$ be the initial current (by [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^8cd332]]).

$$
\begin{align}
\frac{dq}{dt}&=\frac{V\cancel{C}}{R\cancel{C}}\,e^{-t/RC} =I_{0}\,e^{-t/RC} \\
\end{align}
$$

For the equivalent equations for discharging, just flip the original sign of $\frac{q(t)}{C}$ and solve the equation.
`\end{proof}`

### Capacitors in Series and Parallel

> [!thm] Capacitors in Series
>
> Two capacitors in **series** with [[#^3b3908]] $C_{1},C_{2}$ have an have an **equivalent capacitance** $C$. $$\frac{1}{C}=\frac{1}{C_{1}}+\frac{1}{C_{2}}$$

^0e85a6

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^0e85a6]]
By [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^03a977]], the voltage across each capacitor will sum to the total voltage $V=Q / C$.

$$
\begin{align}
V=\frac{\cancel{Q}}{C}=\sum \frac{\cancel{Q}}{C_{n}}&=\sum \frac{1}{C_{n}}\\
\end{align}
$$
`\end{proof}`

> [!thm] Capacitors in Parallel
>
> Two capacitors in **parallel** with [[#^3b3908]] $C_{1},C_{2}$ have an have an **equivalent capacitance** $C$. $$C=C_{1}+C_{2}$$

`\begin{proof}`
By [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^03a977]], capacitor will have the same voltage across their plates, and the charges across each capacitor $Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots$ will sum to the total charge $Q=CV$.
$$
\begin{align}
Q=\cancel{V}C =\sum \cancel{V_{n}}C_{n}&=\sum C_{n}\\
\end{align}
$$
`\end{proof}`

## Energy of a Capacitor

> [!thm] Energy of a Capacitor
> The maximum energy stored inside a capacitor with [[#^3b3908]] $C$ given a [[2.3.1 - Ohm's Law#^23952c]] $V$ is
>
> $$
> U_{\text{max}}=\frac{1}{2}{CV^{2}}
> $$

^b84e62

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^b84e62]]
The capacitor gains energy as it charges. Let the charge of the capacitor be $q$ at any given moment, with a maximum charge of $Q$. The [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^e395c7]] $dW$ done by a charge $dq$ is

$$
dW=V\,dq=\frac{q}{C}\,dq
$$

By the [[1.5 - Work and Energy#^2bd330]], the total energy stored is the total work done.

$$
\begin{align}
W&=\int_{0}^{Q}{\frac{q}{C}}\,dq =\frac{1}{2}{\frac{Q^{2}}{C}} =\frac{1}{2}CV^{2}
\end{align}
$$

`\end{proof}`
