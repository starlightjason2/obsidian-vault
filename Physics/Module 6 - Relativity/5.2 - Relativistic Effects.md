# 5.2 - Relativistic Effects

Special relativity follows logically from the following *postulates*.

1. The laws of physics are the same for all inertial reference frames.
2. The speed of light is constant for all inertial reference frame.

The following definitions are useful for the mathematical consequences of special relativity.

> [!def] Rest Frame
> The inertial frame of any observer(s) in space and time.

^rest-frame

> [!def] Light-speed Ratio
> Velocity as a fraction of light-speed. Let $v$ be the velocity of an object, and let $c$ be the speed of light.
>$$
>\beta=\frac{v}{c}
>$$
>

^light-speed-ratio

> [!def] Lorentz Factor
> The scaling factor for most relativistic effects, as a function of [[#^light-speed-ratio]] $\beta$. Notice that $\gamma$ has a lower bound of $\gamma=1$ and approaches $\infty$ as $v\to c$.
>
>$$
>\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}}
>$$
>

^lorentz-factor

## Time Dilation

> [!def] Proper Time
The **proper time** $\Delta t_{0}$ of an event is measured in it's [[#^rest-frame]]. The event *starts and ends at the same place* in this frame.

^be734e

> [!thm] Time Dilation
> Consider two frames in relative motion with a [[#^lorentz-factor]] of $\gamma$. An observer at rest in one frame will measure a duration $\Delta t$ equal to the [[#^be734e]] duration $\Delta t_0$ of the moving frame scaled by $\gamma$.
>
>
>$$
>\Delta t=\Delta t_{0}\,\gamma
>$$

^time-dilation

`\begin{proof}`@[[#^time-dilation]] ^59fc7f

We consider a thought experiment to derive the consequences to space and time. Consider an observer on a train moving at speed $v$ relative to an individual at the station.

On the train, we measure proper time $t_0$ by bouncing light up-and-down the railcar (which has height $h$). The light travels a distance $\Delta x_{0}=2\,h$ at the speed of light $c$.

$$
\Delta x_{0}=2h=\,c\,t_{0}
$$

^2c9a2e

Meanwhile at the station, an observer sees the light travel $\Delta x=c\,t$. However, the motion of the light is now diagonal due to the motion of the train, and so

$$
\Delta x=2\sqrt{h^{2}+\left( \frac{1}{2}v\,t \right)^{2}}=c\,t
$$

^eb10e9

Or solving for $h$, we can say $h^{2}=\frac{1}{4}t^{2}\,(c^{2}-v^{2})$. We can now substitute [[#^2c9a2e]] to get an equation relating $t$ and $t_{0}$.

$$
\begin{align}
 \cancel{\frac{1}{4}}c^{2}\,t_0^{2}&=\frac{t^{2}}{\cancel{4}}(c^{2}-v^{2}) \\
c^{2}\,t_0^{2}&=t^{2}(c^{2}-v^{2}) \\
t^{2}&=\frac{c^{2}}{(c^{2}-v^{2})}\,t_0^{2}
\end{align}
$$

$$
t^{2}=\frac{t_0^{2}}{\sqrt{1-\left( \frac{v}{c} \right)^{2}}}
$$

^d2baf0

`\end{proof}`

## Length Contraction

> [!def] Proper Length
> The **proper length** $\Delta x_{0}$ is the displacement measured in the [[#^rest-frame]]. For an object, it's the length *measured in the object's frame*.

^proper-length

> [!thm] Length Contraction
> Consider two frames in relative motion with a [[#^lorentz-factor]] of $\gamma$. An observer at rest in one frame will measure a length $\Delta x$ equal to the [[#^proper-length]] $\Delta x_0$ of the moving frame scaled by $\gamma^{-1}$.
>
>$$
>\Delta x=\frac{\Delta x_{0}}{\gamma}
>$$

^length-contraction

`\begin{proof}`
Resume the thought experiment in [[#^59fc7f|Proof of Time Dilation]]. This time, we sit at the station and measure the length of the train by timing it's passing, which starts and ends at the origin in our frame. Therefore, we use the **station's time** $\Delta t_{S}$ as the **proper time** for the passing event, and measure a length of $\Delta x_{S}=v\,\Delta t_{S}$.

Meanwhile, the passengers on the front of the train see the station moving at $-v$, and watch the station first pass the origin and then the back of the train. Their clock measures $\Delta t_{T}$ for the event, and so they calculate a length of $\Delta x_{T}=v\,\Delta t_{T}$. This is the **proper length** of the train (the length as measured in its frame). Therefore,

$$
\frac{\Delta x_{S}}{\Delta x_{T}}=\frac{\Delta t_{S}}{\Delta t_{T}}
$$

^2b33a7

By [[#^time-dilation]], the train's time is $t_{T}=\gamma\,t_{S}$, where . Substitute into [[#^2b33a7]],

$$
\Delta x_{S}=\Delta x_{T}\left( \frac{\cancel{\Delta t_{S}}}{\gamma\,\cancel{\Delta t_{T}}} \right)
$$

Because $\Delta x_{T}$ is the proper length, we can denote it as $\Delta x_{0}$, and $\Delta x_{S}$ as an arbitrary $x$.

$$
\Delta x=\frac{\Delta x_{0}}{\gamma}
$$
